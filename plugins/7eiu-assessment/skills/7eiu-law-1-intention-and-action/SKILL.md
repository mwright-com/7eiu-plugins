## The law

Intention comes first, then action, and what you get out is governed by how clearly you knew what you wanted going in.

The book's image is a man who walks into a hardware store and asks for a drill. He does not need a drill. He needs a hole, because he wants to hang a shelf, because his wife asked him to. Sell him the drill and you have solved a problem he does not have. The second image is the mirror: the machine reflects back the clarity of the mind in front of it, so a fuzzy intention returns a fuzzy result, fast and confident and beautifully finished. Both images say the same thing from two sides. The person asking is the constraint.

In a business, this law governs leadership. It covers how decisions get made and by whom, whether the owner can name the one outcome that matters, and the quality of the instructions every other function is built out of.

## What this law is looking for in a business

You are listening for how the owner talks about what they want, and for what happens to that want on its way down to the people doing the work.

**Signals the law is being obeyed:**

- The owner finishes the sentence "the single most important outcome I need is ___" without a run-up, and finishes it the same way twice.
- The twelve-month goal comes back in the same words from a second person on the team.
- Somebody can tell you who decides what, and where a decision goes when it is above their line.
- Instructions carry the why. "Get the guide downloaders from last week onto a call" instead of "send a newsletter."
- The owner names context an outsider would never guess, and names it without being pushed.
- There is a written version of that context somewhere: an onboarding doc, a page of standards, a file a new hire actually gets.
- When a tool or a contractor gives back something wrong, the owner's first move is to ask again with more of the truth in the question.
- Work gets commissioned against an outcome, and there is a plain way to tell later whether the outcome happened.

**Signals the law is being broken:**

- The owner cannot finish the sentence "the single most important outcome I need is ___". They give you three answers, or they give you a category.
- The team gets roughly-right instructions and interprets them, and everyone has made peace with that.
- Two people on the team describe the twelve-month goal differently, and neither thinks it is a problem.
- AI use is deliverable-shaped. "Write me an email." "Make me a job description." Nobody asked for the hole.
- The first answer from a tool is treated as the deliverable. It gets edited for tone and shipped, or it gets judged as bad and the tool gets blamed.
- Nobody has told the tools the context only this company has: the customers, the margins, the last three months, the reason the business started.
- Decisions wait on the owner, and the owner does not know that they are waiting.
- Rework is normal and is described as normal. "We usually go around a couple of times on that."
- The owner says a version of "I'll know it when I see it" about work they are paying for.
- Instructions travel by memory. What was asked for on Monday cannot be produced on Friday.

## What to ask

Round 2 of the intake covers this law. Ask the opening question, listen, and take the probes the answer earns.

From the intake:

- When something needs deciding, how does it get decided, and by whom?
- What is the one outcome that matters most right now? Say it in one sentence.
- Where does the team get instructions that are clear, and where do they get instructions that are roughly right and have to be interpreted?
- When you or your team use AI tools, what do you typically ask for? Get an actual example.
- What context about this business would an outsider never guess?

Put the book's own diagnostic to the owner directly, out loud, and let the silence sit:

> Finish this sentence for me. *The single most important thing I need from this is ______.*

If they cannot finish it, that is the finding. Write it down as what happened, not as a judgment.

Probes worth adding:

- Ask the same one-outcome question of a second person on the team, separately, and compare the two answers word for word.
- Ask for the last thing they asked a tool for, verbatim, and then ask what they were going to do with the result. The distance between those two answers is the whole law.
- Ask what got redone last month, and who decided it needed redoing.
- Ask what a new hire has to be told that is written down nowhere.

## Opportunity patterns

Every recommendation here has to be startable inside a month. If it needs a quarter and a consultant, it belongs to a different document.

**1. Name the one outcome.**
Missing when the owner answers the one-sentence question with a list, or with one number that has three numbers hiding inside it.
- *One person:* write one sentence on an index card, put it where you work, and check every commitment against it for thirty days.
- *Ten people:* say it at the start of the weekly meeting, in the same words, until someone else says it back to you unprompted.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* write it into the operating cadence so each function states how its quarter serves that one outcome, and so an acquired team inherits it in week one.

**2. Write down the context only this company has.**
Missing when the owner can tell you something surprising about the business in the interview and it exists nowhere on paper.
- *One person:* one page. Who buys, what they did before you, what they are afraid of, what you refuse to do. Paste it at the top of any tool session.
- *Ten people:* keep it as a living document the team can add to, and hand it to a new person on day one alongside the payroll forms.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* keep one per business unit, and make writing it part of what a new acquisition produces before anyone rebuilds its marketing.

**3. Turn deliverable-requests into outcome-requests.**
Missing when every example of AI use in the intake starts with a verb and a noun. Write me an email. Build me a deck.
- *One person:* before you ask for anything, say what you want to be true when it works. Ask for that instead.
- *Ten people:* run one session where the team rewrites five of their real requests as outcomes and compares the results side by side.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* put the rewrite into how work gets commissioned, so a brief without a stated outcome gets sent back.

**4. Teach the ask-again loop.**
Missing when the first answer is either shipped or used as evidence that the tools do not work.
- *One person:* a rule for yourself. Three rounds before you judge anything.
- *Ten people:* have one person walk the team through a real four-round conversation on a live piece of work, showing what each answer revealed about the question.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* make the loop part of how new people are trained, so the habit arrives with them instead of being retrofitted.

**5. State the intention before any generated work gets commissioned.**
Missing when a vague instruction has already turned into a large amount of confident, misdirected work.
- *One person:* one line at the top of the request. What is this for.
- *Ten people:* the same line as a required field on whatever the team already uses to assign work, so nothing starts without it.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* review a sample of finished work each month against the intention it was commissioned under, and fix the instruction rather than the output.

**6. Give decisions a named owner.**
Missing when the answer to "how does it get decided" is a shrug, or is always the owner.
- *One person:* write the two or three decisions you keep re-deciding, and decide them once.
- *Ten people:* name who decides what, in writing, including what they can decide without you.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* carry that map into due diligence, so you can see whose judgment you are buying and what happens when they leave.

## The AI angle

Describe what the tools do. Never name a product, a company, or a version number. A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

These tools have made the distance between intention and result very short. That is the whole change. There used to be a slow, expensive process in the middle that caught vagueness and forced a person to think it through. Take that out and a rough instruction becomes a large amount of finished, confident, misdirected work in an afternoon. A vague intention costs more now than it did when the middle was slow, because more gets built on it before anybody stops to look. When an owner tells you rework is normal here, go back to what was asked for rather than to what came back.

The context an owner holds is the only thing separating a generic answer from a useful one. The tools know a great deal about the world and nothing about this business: not the customer, not the margins, not the last three months, not the reason any of it started. Owners who get results are telling the tools the truth about their situation in detail. When you see a company getting bland output, look first at what it is not saying.

The tools also work in the other direction, which is worth telling an owner who is stuck. Someone who cannot see their own intention clearly can put what they do have in front of a tool and be argued with. "Here is who I think pays me, here is what they do instead today, here is why I think they are unhappy, now push back." That gets a sparring partner instead of a horoscope, and it is available to the owner tonight.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 1 section

The heading is `PRINCIPLE 1: Intent & Action`.

One to three short paragraphs. Shorter is honest when the intake was thin. The benchmark's Principle 1 is a single paragraph and it works. Do not pad this section to match the length of the fat ones later in the document.

Build it from the four moves: the observation, the law applied, the opportunity, the honest gap. Not all four have to appear, and they do not have to appear in that order. Open on something the owner actually said. Their words, close to verbatim. If you have their one-sentence outcome, that is almost always the opening.

Hedge to what you learned. "It seems." "There may be an opportunity." "From my vantage point." Use first and second person throughout, "I" and "you", and use first names for the people the owner named.

Vocabulary this law owns: the mirror, the drill and the hole, intention before action, the conversation, the ask-again loop, clarity, the one outcome.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| Law | Its images |
|---|---|
| 2. Big and Small | the snowflake and the glacier, translation, the pattern, figurative scale |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 4. Active and Passive Marketing | active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil |
| 5. Wins and Losses | hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the continuous scale |
| 6. Rhythm and Fit | rhythm, the swing, the kite, the wind, the tide, the arc, fit |
| 7. Karma and Value | the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf, giving and extracting, the bay, the net |

That is the whole table minus Law 1's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

You may name another law by name when a finding hands off to it, in one clause, without arguing that finding again in your own imagery.

This section often carries the framing paragraph's verdict. When intention is clear, everything downstream in the document reads differently, so what you conclude here sets the reader's footing for the other six. If the owner is clear, say so early and move on. If the owner is not, this section is the one the assessment turns on, and the rest of the document should stop pretending otherwise.

## Worked example

The benchmark's PRINCIPLE 1 section, in full:

> If you start with an intention of caring, then that should guide your messaging - both internally and externally. What you care about will be up to the team, but caring actions, both inside and outside the company will serve to make the challenges of growth more manageable.

One short paragraph was the right call. The framing paragraph had already judged the first six laws as satisfied by the fact that the company was worth acquiring, so there was nothing to prove here. The differentiator the owner named was a team that cares about each other. This section's only job was to take that stated value and make it the intention everything else gets measured against, which is what the first sentence does. There was no honest gap to write, because nothing was missing. Anything longer would have been the author performing thoroughness he had not earned.

**Illustration only. The company below is invented for teaching purposes. It is not a client and nothing in it happened.**

For a fictional three-person commercial landscaping outfit, verdict `thin`:

> When I asked what the one outcome was for the next twelve months, you gave me three: more commercial contracts, less residential, and getting off the mower yourself. Any of the three is a fine goal. Together they pull in different directions, and I think that is what Dana was describing when she said the schedule "changes on her by Wednesday." Three good instructions arriving at once look the same from her seat as one bad one.
>
> The move I would start with this month is picking the one and saying it to both of them in the same words every Monday until one of them says it back to you. Once that sentence exists, the property manager list you mentioned becomes easy to sort, because you will know what you are sorting for.
>
> I did not get far enough into how work gets assigned to say anything useful about it:
>
> - *When a bid goes out, who writes it, and what do they have in front of them when they start?*
> - *What did Dana have to figure out on her own in her first month that nobody wrote down?*

## What you hand back

The five-part block from the chapter-skill contract:

1. `verdict`. One of `strong`, `working`, `thin`, `not observed`. This is what the framing paragraph and the Quick Read rank on.
2. `section`. The prose for the full assessment, ready to sit under the `PRINCIPLE 1: Intent & Action` heading.
3. `quick_read`. Two or three sentences, one observation and one opportunity, written fresh rather than cut down from the section.
4. `opportunities`. The concrete moves you named, listed so the orchestrator can rank them and Principle 7 can walk them in order.
5. `gaps`. What you could not assess, with the questions the owner should answer. These go in the document, not into a private note.

Optionally `blocked_on`. The law whose finding has to move first, when this law's recommendation is right but cannot be started until something else changes. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

How to pick the verdict for this law:

- `strong`. The owner names one outcome without a run-up, a second person says it back the same way, and the context that makes this business itself is written somewhere a new hire or a tool can be handed.
- `working`. The owner is clear, and the clarity stops at the owner. Instructions downstream are roughly right and get interpreted.
- `thin`. The one-sentence question produces a list, a category, or a pause. Everything downstream inherits that.
- `not observed`. Round 2 did not happen, or happened so fast that you have one answer and no probes. Say that plainly and write the questions.

Optionally `diagram`, when this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement. Propose it and let the orchestrator decide.

Filled in for the landscaping illustration:

```
verdict: thin

section: [the two paragraphs and the italic questions above]

quick_read: You named three outcomes for the next twelve months, and Dana
is working from all three at once. Pick one, say it every Monday in the same
words, and the property manager list you mentioned starts sorting itself.

opportunities:
  - Name the one outcome and repeat it weekly until it comes back to you.
  - Write down what a new person has to be told that currently lives in your head.

gaps:
  - How bids get written and what the writer starts from.
  - What Dana had to work out on her own in her first month.
```

## Self-check

Run this before handing the block back.

- No AI product name, company name, or version number appears anywhere.
- Every claim traces to something marked `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` in the intake. Anything else is cut or turned into a question.
- No invented person, client, number, statistic, or anecdote.
- Only Law 1's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
- The section opens on something the owner actually said, in their words.
- Every recommendation can be started inside a month by a company this size.
- The section is as long as what you learned and no longer. If the intake was thin, one paragraph is the honest answer.
- It does not end on a manufactured takeaway. It ends on the content, a question, or what happens next.
- First and second person throughout. "I" and "you". No "the client", no "stakeholders".
- Claims are hedged where the evidence is thin, and specific where it is not.
- Any gap is written into the section as a gap, with italic questions, rather than left out.
- The verdict matches the section. A `thin` verdict with three confident paragraphs under it means one of the two is wrong.
