## The law

Wins and losses are the same event measured at two points on one scale, and you can move along the scale.

The book's image is temperature. Hot and cold are not two substances fighting each other. There is one continuous scale, and what you call cold is less of what you call hot. Ice and steam are the same water. The second image is the coin: a win and a loss are two sides of it, and the coin has value either way, so turning it over does not change what it is worth.

This law governs the customer relationships the company already has. Who is warm, who is cooling, who left, and whether anybody learns anything from how any of it goes.

## What this law is looking for in a business

Signals the law is being obeyed:

- The owner can name three customers who are thrilled, and can say what made each one thrilled.
- The owner can name which customers are cooling, and can say what started the cooling.
- A customer leaves and somebody writes down why, in a place a second person can read.
- A good month gets examined the same week it happens, and somebody says out loud what caused it.
- There is a habit around failure that runs on a calendar rather than on how bad the failure felt.
- Somebody brings a customer objection back into the company, and a change gets made because of it.
- The change gets shown to other customers as evidence of how the company adapts.
- Customer history sits somewhere the whole team can see, not only with whoever handled the account.
- A win from last quarter has already been turned into something the team runs on purpose.

Signals the law is being broken:

- Nobody can name why the good customers are good. They are just the good ones.
- Nobody can name which customers would leave at the first opportunity.
- A customer leaves and no one writes down why. The account closes and the reason goes with it.
- A good month happens and no one asks what caused it. It gets enjoyed and then it gets forgotten.
- The company is coasting on a win from three years ago that has gone cold, and nobody has taken the temperature since.
- Failure gets punished, so losses get hidden and the same failures repeat invisibly.
- Customer stories exist only in the heads of whoever handled the account.
- Wins and losses get sorted by category rather than by customer. The company knows which product line is winning and cannot say which customer is.
- The only record of a relationship is the invoice history.
- When something works, the explanation is luck, timing, or a person's gut.

## What to ask

From Round 6 of the intake interview:

- Who are your success stories, and why did those work?
- Which customers have been difficult, and why?
- Are there customers who would switch at the first opportunity? Do you know which?
- When you lose one, what happens? Does anyone write down why?
- When something works well, does anyone figure out why it worked?
- Where do customer stories live right now, if anywhere?

Probes to add:

- Name your last real loss. Walk me through what happened after it. Who talked about it, and how long after?
- Name your best month in the last two years. What did you do differently that month, and are you still doing it?
- Is there a customer who would take a call from a prospect and speak for you? Who?
- When a customer complains, where does the complaint end up?

The two questions the benchmark put to the client, which belong in the document verbatim when the intake does not answer them:

- *Who are the success stories? Why?*
- *What clients have been problematic? Why?*

Mark every answer `[said]`, `[doc]`, `[observed]` or `[gap]`. A guess about who the happy customers probably are is a `[gap]`, and it goes into the document as a question.

## Opportunity patterns

**1. Both autopsies on a calendar.**
Missing when the answer to "what happened after that" is a feeling rather than a meeting. A loss autopsy runs a few days after every real failure. A win autopsy runs after every real success, and it is the one that gets skipped.

- One-person shop: two recurring appointments on your own calendar, thirty minutes each. After a loss, write the answer to what this revealed that you could not see from inside. After a win, write the answer to what the repeatable part was.
- Team of ten: same two habits, but the person closest to the account writes the first draft and the team reads it. Keep both in one running file so the reasons stack up in one place.
- Scaling or acquiring: run the loss autopsy on the deals that did not close as well as the customers who left, and run it at every site you take on before you standardize anything.

**2. Find out whether you have superfans.**
Missing when the owner believes some customers love the company and cannot name one, or names one and has never asked that customer why.

- One-person shop: pick your five warmest customers and ask each one what almost stopped them from hiring you and what made them stay. One call each, this month.
- Team of ten: same five calls, split across the people who own the accounts, then put the answers side by side and look for the sentence that keeps coming back. That sentence is the story you tell internally first and externally second.
- Scaling or acquiring: identify the superfans at each location before you change anything there, because they are the ones who will tell you what you are about to break.

**3. Record the objections, act on them, then show the change.**
Missing when customers have been sharing problems for a year and nothing in the company has moved because of it.

- One-person shop: one page, one list. Every objection or complaint you hear goes on it with a date. Once a month, pick the one that appears most often and fix it. Then tell the customers who raised it what you changed.
- Team of ten: the list has to be somewhere everyone writes to. Give one person the monthly job of reading it and naming the fix. Send the fix to existing customers and put it in front of prospects as evidence of how you adapt.
- Scaling or acquiring: make the objection list part of what you inherit in a deal, and use the first fix as the proof to the acquired customers that the new owner listens.

**4. Get customer history out of individual heads.**
Missing when the answer to "what is the story with that account" is a person's name.

- One-person shop: one line per customer after every meaningful conversation, in whatever you already have open. It does not need software to start.
- Team of ten: a shared record with the history, the objections, and the reason each departed customer left. If the company has no customer relationship software that can surface these stories, something workable can be running inside a month.
- Scaling or acquiring: the customer record is part of what you are buying. Ask for it before the deal closes, and if it does not exist, budget the first sixty days for building it out of the heads of the people you kept.

**5. Turn an understood win into a recipe.**
Missing when the owner can point at a great month and cannot say what caused it.

- One-person shop: take one good month and put the record of it in front of a tool. The sales, the messages, the timing, what you changed. Ask for the pattern. Then run one piece of the pattern again on purpose.
- Team of ten: whoever ran the winning work writes the steps down while it is fresh, and the next person runs those steps rather than starting over.
- Scaling or acquiring: a written recipe is what makes a win travel to the second site. An unwritten one stays with the person who got lucky.

**6. Take the temperature of the old win.**
Missing when the company is still describing itself by something that worked three years ago.

- One-person shop: name the thing you believe is carrying you. Then check whether it produced anything in the last ninety days.
- Team of ten: put one number on it and look at that number quarterly. If it has cooled, say so before it becomes a surprise.
- Scaling or acquiring: check whether the acquired company's stated strength is a current win or an old one, because the price usually assumes the former.

Every recommendation you write has to be startable inside a month. If it needs a hire, a budget cycle, or a system nobody has bought yet, shrink it until it starts on a Tuesday.

## The AI angle

Describe what the tools do and never what they are called.

The loss autopsy is the one the owner's mind fights. When something fails it hurts, and the mind protects itself by looking away, by blaming the market or the timing or the customer, by finding any reason except the true one. A machine has no ego. It will run the cold examination without flinching from the failure, and it will not flatter the owner the way the owner flatters themselves. That is what makes it useful a few days after a loss, when a human partner is still being kind.

The win autopsy used to be out of reach for a different reason. Understanding why a good month happened required analysis nobody had time for. Now the owner can sit down with the record of what happened and have a tool help find the pattern in an hour. The win stops being luck and becomes a recipe, and a recipe can be run again on purpose.

For a company with no customer relationship software that can surface these stories, something workable can be up and running quickly. This is the recommendation the benchmark made, and it is worth making plainly. The system is no longer the hard part. A tool with no ego will sit down with the record and run the examination the owner's own mind fights, as often as the owner is willing to run it.

Never name an AI product, company, or version number. Write "AI tools", "these types of solutions", "a tool that can". A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 5 section

Heading: `PRINCIPLE 5: Winning & Losing`

Length: two to four short paragraphs.

Use the four moves from the benchmark spec, in whatever order the material wants: the observation, the law applied, the opportunity, the honest gap.

This is very often the section where the honest gap belongs. Owners routinely have not looked at their customer base this way, and the interview will surface that within two questions. When it happens, do not fill the hole with a guess and do not skip the section. Say what you did learn. Say what you did not hear. Put the questions to the owner in italics and let him answer them. The benchmark did exactly this, and the section is stronger for it than a confident invention would have been.

Vocabulary this law owns: hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the scale, warming and cooling, degree. Use one image with weight rather than all of them lightly.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| Law | Its images |
|---|---|
| 1. Intention and Action | the mirror, the drill and the hole, intention before action, the conversation |
| 2. Big and Small | the snowflake and the glacier, translation, the pattern, figurative scale |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 4. Active and Passive Marketing | active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil |
| 6. Rhythm and Fit | rhythm, the swing, the kite, the wind, the tide, the arc, fit |
| 7. Karma and Value | the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf, giving and extracting, the bay, the net |

That is the whole table minus Law 5's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and this law's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

You may name another law when a finding hands off to it, in one clause, without arguing that finding again in your own imagery.

First person and second person. "I", "you". Hedge to what you actually learned: "it seems", "I suspect", "there may be an opportunity". Short paragraphs. End on the content or on a question.

## Worked example

The benchmark's PRINCIPLE 5 section read in full:

> Although I did learn about which EHR applications are "winning" and which are "losing", and, to some degree, how well the Clear RCM team works together, I didn't hear much discussion on that level in terms of client base.
>
> - *Who are the success stories? Why?*
> - *What clients have been problematic? Why?*
>
> That discussion was largely limited to the type of EHR they were on. But, are there clients that are thrilled about Clear RCM services enough to be brand evangelists? Are there clients that would switch at the first opportunity?
>
> If the company does have superfans, tell that story both internally and externally. Likewise, if there are clients that you are not in close contact with, or that have been regularly sharing objections or problems, record that. Take action on them, then share these beneficial changes with existing clients and prospects as examples of how Clear RCM adapts.
>
> If you don't have CRM software that can currently bubble those stories to the surface, AI would be well-suited to getting something like that up-and-running quickly.

What it is doing:

- It opens by crediting what was learned. The winning and losing EHR applications, and how the team works together, both get named before anything is said to be missing.
- The honest gap is the spine of the section, not an aside at the end. The whole section is built on what was not heard.
- The two italic questions are handed to the owner to answer. They are not answered, softened, or filled in with a plausible guess.
- The words "winning" and "losing" are in quotation marks because they are the client's words for the EHR platforms, kept verbatim.
- It names precisely where the discussion did happen, the type of EHR, so the reader can see the shape of what was missing rather than being told he missed something.
- The two follow-up questions about brand evangelists and about switching at the first opportunity turn the gap into something the owner can go and check by Friday.
- The superfan recommendation loops the finding back to Principle 4's garden. Law 5 owns the finding, which customers are the success stories and why, and Law 4 owns publishing them into the garden. The benchmark says tell the story internally and externally, which is the marketing law's work, and it does not re-argue the marketing law here.
- The objection recommendation runs in three beats: record it, act on it, then show existing clients and prospects the change as evidence of how the company adapts.
- Nothing is invented. There is no claim about a client the interview did not produce.
- The closing sentence names a capability rather than a product. "AI would be well-suited" and nothing more specific, which is why the paragraph will still be true in five years.

Now an illustration. **The company below is fictional, invented for this file, and must never be presented to a client as a real engagement.**

Halvorsen Remodeling, a twelve-person residential remodeling contractor. Verdict: `working`.

> You know why the good jobs go well. You told me the kitchen on Arbor Street was the best job you ran last year, and then you told me why without pausing: the homeowner picked finishes before demolition started, Ray was on it from the first day to the last, and nobody had to make a decision on the floor. That is a win you understand, which puts you ahead of where I usually find a shop your size. It is not yet a recipe. Nobody wrote those three conditions down, so the next crew that gets a job like that will have to be lucky in the same way rather than run it the same way.
>
> The cold side is where I did not hear much. You said there were two jobs last year that went badly and that you would rather not relive them, which I understand. But nothing was written down after either one, and the men who were on those crews are still here.
>
> - *What did each of those two jobs reveal that you could not see while it was happening?*
> - *Which of your last twenty homeowners would take a call from a prospect and speak for you?*
>
> There may be an opportunity in putting both examinations on the calendar rather than leaving them to how the week feels. A short session a few days after a job goes wrong, and the same session after a job goes right. A tool has no ego about your worst job and will ask the cold questions your own mind will fight you on. Start with the Arbor Street kitchen, because it is warm and recent and nobody will get defensive, and by the time the next bad job arrives the habit already exists.

## What you hand back

Return the five-part block from the chapter-skill contract. Add `diagram` only if this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement.

Add `blocked_on` when the recommendation here is right but cannot be started until something else changes. It names the law whose finding has to move first. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

Filled in for the fictional remodeling illustration:

**verdict**
`working`

**section**
The four paragraphs above, ready to drop under `PRINCIPLE 5: Winning & Losing`.

**quick_read**
You can say precisely why the Arbor Street kitchen went well, which is more than most shops your size can do, but nobody wrote those conditions down and nothing at all was written after the two jobs that went badly. Two standing sessions, one after a job goes wrong and one after a job goes right, would turn both into something the next crew can use.

**opportunities**
1. Write the three conditions that made the Arbor Street job work into a one-page starting checklist for the next kitchen.
2. Put a loss session and a win session on the calendar as standing appointments, thirty minutes each.
3. Call five recent homeowners and ask what almost stopped them from hiring you and what made them stay.
4. Start one shared file for job history so the reasons do not stay with whoever ran the crew.

**gaps**
I did not learn why the two bad jobs went bad, and I did not hear whether any homeowner would speak for you to a prospect.

- *What did each of those two jobs reveal that you could not see while it was happening?*
- *Which of your last twenty homeowners would take a call from a prospect and speak for you?*
- *Is there a homeowner you would rather not call back? What happened there?*

## Self-check

Before handing the block back, walk this list.

1. No AI product name, company name, or version number anywhere in the section, the quick read, or the opportunities.
2. What you did learn is credited before anything is named as missing.
3. Every gap is written into the document as an italic question, not filled in with a plausible guess about who the good customers are.
4. Only Law 5's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
5. Both autopsies are covered where the material allows, not only the loss one. The win autopsy is the one that gets dropped.
6. Every recommendation can be started within a month, without a hire or a budget cycle.
7. The section speaks about customers by relationship, not only by product line or platform category.
8. Any word in quotation marks is the owner's own word.
9. Nothing in the section is an inference. Every claim traces to a `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` entry.
10. If the superfan finding hands off to Principle 4, it does so in a clause. Law 5 owns the finding, which customers are the success stories and why. Law 4 owns publishing them into the garden, and this section does not re-argue the marketing law.
11. First person and second person throughout. No "the client", no "the organization".
12. The last sentence ends on content, a next step, or a question, and not on a manufactured takeaway.
