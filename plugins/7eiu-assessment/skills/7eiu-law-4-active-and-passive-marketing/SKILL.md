## The law

Nothing creates by itself, so a business that generates new customers is always running two agents at once: one that goes out, and one that draws in.

The book's image is the seed and the soil. A seed alone is not a plant. Soil alone is not a plant. It takes the active agent that arrives carrying the blueprint and the receptive agent that holds it and feeds it before anything grows. In marketing those two agents have plainer names. Active marketing is a fire: it gives heat as long as you feed it and goes out the moment you stop. Passive marketing is a garden: it takes a season to grow, and then it feeds you every year with less tending. The hunter eats tonight. The gardener eats for years.

In a business, this law governs marketing. It covers how the company reaches strangers, what the company has built that draws strangers in without anyone working, and the loop where each side feeds the other: reach starts relationships, relationships served well become reputation, reputation makes the next reach easier.

## What this law is looking for in a business

There is one diagnostic that sorts everything else in this section. **If the owner stopped working for a month, would new customers still find them?** Ask it, then listen to which way the answer falls. A no puts you on the chaser side. A yes with a quiet phone puts you on the gardener side. Everything below is how each side shows up in an intake.

**Signals the law is being obeyed:**

- The owner can walk you through the last three new customers and at least one of them arrived without anybody reaching out.
- Something the company published a year ago is still bringing in people who mention it by name.
- There is a real answer to "what do you have sitting in the open that a stranger can find and use," and it is more than a brochure site.
- Outreach is specific. The owner can tell you why this person, at this moment, would care.
- Somebody who was reached cold last quarter came back on their own this quarter.
- New prospects arrive already knowing something about the company, so the first conversation starts partway in.
- Both sides have a named owner and a standing slot in the week, even in a two-person shop.
- The published work is made out of what the company actually knows, and a customer would recognize it as theirs.
- Reach and reputation are visibly connected: the outreach points at the assets, and the assets make the next call warmer.

**Signals the law is being broken, on the chaser side:**

- The honest answer to the month-off question is no, and the owner knows it before finishing the sentence.
- Every dollar of revenue costs a fresh dollar of effort. Volume goes up only when hours go up.
- Nothing the company owns works while they sleep. There is no asset a stranger can find at midnight.
- The owner is the pipeline. If they get sick, new business stops that week.
- They have built a job rather than a business, and the intake makes that plain without anyone naming it.
- Revenue is flat while the owner is working harder than last year.
- The website is a business card. Address, phone, a paragraph about values, nothing a stranger would take away with them.
- Outreach volume keeps rising and reply rates keep falling, because specificity got traded for reach.
- Case stories exist in the owner's head and have never been written down for anyone outside the company.

**Signals the law is being broken, on the gardener side:**

- A fine reputation and a quiet phone. Referrals are the whole pipeline and referrals are down.
- They are magnetic and waiting. The assets are good and nobody is pointing anyone at them.
- Nobody goes out to start the relationships the reputation could easily keep.
- The owner describes reaching out as something that feels like begging, or beneath them.
- Growth stalled at a number that has not moved in two years and nobody can say why.
- There is no list. No record of who has been talked to, what was said, and when to talk again.
- Good work gets published and then nothing happens with it. No follow-up, no invitation, no next step on the page.
- New entrants with less experience are winning work because they asked and the company did not.

## What to ask

Round 5 of the intake covers this law. Lead with the diagnostic. Everything else in the round follows from the answer, and the follow-up questions you choose depend on which side the answer put you on.

> **If you stopped working for a month, would new customers still find you?**

Ask it, then stop talking. The pause is the question. Whatever comes back, that is the finding, and it tells you which half of the round to spend your time on.

The rest of Round 5:

- How does a new customer find you today? Walk through the last three.
- What are you doing to reach out right now, and how is it going?
- What do you have sitting in the open that a stranger can find and use? Site, guides, case studies, videos, a newsletter, anything.
- Is your market filling up with new entrants, and what kind?

Probes worth adding:

- Pull up the site while they are on the call and ask what a stranger can take away from it. Watch what they do with the silence.
- Ask what they know that their customers keep getting wrong. That answer is usually the first thing to build in the garden, and it comes out fast because it annoys them.
- Ask for the last outreach message they sent, verbatim, and then ask why they picked that person. If the second answer is thinner than the first, specificity is the problem rather than volume.
- Ask what happens after someone reads or downloads something. If nothing happens, the garden exists and the loop does not.
- If they said referrals, ask who made the last three and whether anyone ever asked those people for another.

## Opportunity patterns

Every recommendation here has to be startable inside a month. Also, do not recommend anything until pattern 1 has been run.

**1. Diagnose the weak side before recommending anything.**
Missing when the assessment reaches for tactics before it has established which side is starving. A chaser handed more outreach advice gets more tired. A gardener handed more content advice gets a bigger, quieter garden.
- *One person:* answer the month-off question out loud, in front of the owner, and say which side you heard.
- *Ten people:* ask the owner and one other person separately, because the owner often knows the answer they wish were true.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* run it per business unit or per acquired team. A company can be a gardener at headquarters and a chaser in every branch.

**2. Plant the garden from week one while active outreach pays the bills.**
Missing when a business a year old is as exhausted as it was at the start, still chasing every customer by hand, with nothing built up that works on its own.
- *One person:* one asset a month, drawn from a question you already answer for free every week. Outreach keeps paying rent while it grows.
- *Ten people:* give one person a standing two hours a week and a named slot on the calendar, so the garden stops being what happens after the busy season.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* make the garden part of what a new unit inherits, so an acquired team gets the assets on day one instead of rebuilding them.

**3. Make outreach specific enough to be relevant without flattening it into bulk mail.**
Missing when reply rates fall as send volume rises, or when the owner cannot say why they picked the person they picked.
- *One person:* twenty people you can name, one reason each, written before you send anything.
- *Ten people:* the owner keeps the judgment about who and why, and the team carries the specificity out. Review a sample of what actually went out each month.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* segment by the thing that makes the message relevant rather than by the thing that is easy to sort on, and check that the segments still hold after a new list arrives.

**4. Build the loop where reach starts relationships, relationships become reputation, and reputation makes the next reach easier.**
Missing when the two sides run as separate programs and neither points at the other.
- *One person:* every outreach message ends by pointing at one thing in the garden, and every asset ends with an invitation to talk.
- *Ten people:* the customer stories that come out of good work go straight into the garden, so served customers become the material that warms the next call.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* wire it into the operating cadence, so support outcomes feed the asset list and the asset list feeds the outreach list on a schedule.

**5. Recruit operators who front a branded experience with the company as the back end.**
Missing when the market is filling with individual providers and the company is competing against them one by one instead of getting ahead of the trend.

Law 6 owns the market read here: who is entering this market, what that means, and who becomes an acquisition target. Law 4 owns it only as a channel decision, when the recommendation is to recruit them. Name Law 6 in a clause and do not argue the market read again in your own imagery.

Say this plainly whenever you write this recommendation: recruiting people to operate under your name touches franchise and licensing law in most US states, so the structure needs a lawyer before anybody signs anything. Write the caveat into the section rather than leaving it for the owner to discover.

- *One person:* write down what the branded experience actually is, in enough detail that somebody who does not work here could deliver it. That page is the product you would be handing an operator, and it is the first real step.
- *Ten people:* have the people who deliver the work today write that page, then have one of them run a job from it as written and fix what the page missed.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* treat the operator network as a channel with its own economics, and check that a real, checkable comparable exists in an adjacent industry before proposing it as a model.

**6. Start with a target of one relationship rather than a campaign.**
Missing when the plan on the table is a launch, a funnel, or a quarter of content, and nobody has had a single new conversation this month.
- *One person:* one person this week. Offer something useful whether they buy or not, and go from there.
- *Ten people:* each person on the team owns one new relationship a week, and the weekly meeting asks for the name.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* keep the target of one alive at the top, so the owner is still starting relationships personally after there is a team to delegate it to.

**7. Turn what the company already knows into something a stranger can hold.**
Missing when the owner explains something valuable in the interview and it exists nowhere a customer could find it.
- *One person:* write the explanation down the way you just said it, publish it, and send it to the next five people who ask.
- *Ten people:* keep a running list of the questions customers actually ask, and work down it.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* audit what each unit knows that the others do not, and publish the overlap once under one name.

## The marketing garden

Give this its own thinking, because it is the thing the owner is most likely to act on.

Active marketing trades your team's time for a prospect's attention. Every conversation costs an hour that only ever gets spent once. A garden does not make that trade. The assets sit in the open, prospects take what is useful to them, and every unsolicited visit is a touchpoint that cost nobody an hour. A prospect is probably not going to close after the first contact. Give them a garden to select from and you get the multiple touchpoints anyway.

The garden hangs off one asset the company already owns, and that anchor is whatever exists rather than whatever would be ideal. In the reference assessment the anchor is the one-page contact form Verity Site Works already has, because it is the only thing in the open with the firm's name on it. An anchor that has to be built before anything can hang off it is not an anchor.

Six slots. The reference assessment's asset menu runs along these lines, and the six hold up across industries because they cover the six different jobs an asset can do.

| Slot | Asset | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| PROOF | Case studies | Proof from a customer who looked like them |
| CAPTURE | Downloadable guides | Something they keep, with your name on it |
| DIAGNOSTIC | Self-assessment surveys | A score on their own operation |
| AUTHORITY | Local market reports | Numbers for their market, not the nation's |
| CADENCE | A newsletter | A standing reason to be heard from |
| ACCESS | Short explainer videos | One real question, answered fast |

**How to fill the six slots for a company that is not the one in the reference.** The slot names are fixed. The assets are not. Choose each one from what this company actually knows and already has, which means the intake record decides the contents. PROOF comes from the work they have already done, so if Round 6 turned up a success story, that is the case study. AUTHORITY comes from numbers they already sit on, so a company that quotes two hundred jobs a year has pricing data nobody else in their market has. DIAGNOSTIC comes from the checklist in the owner's head, the one they run through in the first ten minutes of every site visit. CAPTURE is the thing they explain for free every week. CADENCE is whatever they can actually sustain, and monthly beats weekly if weekly is going to stop in March. ACCESS is the question they are tired of answering.

If a slot has nothing behind it, leave it empty and say so. An empty slot is a finding. A slot filled with something the company does not know is worse than an empty one, because it will show.

The mechanics of that, so the figure and the prose stay in step. The empty slot stays in the figure. It keeps its slot name, it is labelled empty, and it moves to the last position so the diagram does not have a hole in its middle that a reader takes for a rendering fault. The prose then says which slot is empty and what would fill it, in one sentence, so the reader learns it from the writing rather than working it out from a blank card.

Every one of these is buildable with AI tools, and buildable without spending the calendar time it used to take. That is the part worth saying to the owner plainly. A garden used to require a staff and a year. For a small operator, this is the first time it has been possible to build one at all.

## The AI angle

Describe what the tools do. Never name a product, a company, or a version number. A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

For most of the history of small business, the two sides of this law competed for the same scarce resource, which was the owner's time. An hour spent reaching was an hour not spent building, so small operators picked one and starved the other. The tools broke that trade-off. One person can now run both at once, because the machine carries a large share of the labor on each side.

On the active side, the old bargain was that personal meant slow and scalable meant generic. A specific, genuinely relevant message to the right person at the right moment opens a door. The same message sent to a thousand people is clutter. The tools now carry specificity to many people without flattening it, which is what makes reach and relevance stop fighting each other. The judgment stays with the owner. Who to reach and why it would matter to them is still a human call, and it is the part that decides whether any of it works.

On the passive side, a body of useful work published consistently used to take more hours than a small operator had. Now one person can build and sustain that body of work at a volume that used to need a staff. The substance still has to come from the owner. What they know, what they have seen go wrong, the point of view nobody else in their market has. The tools handle the production. They cannot supply the expertise, and an asset built without it reads as empty to exactly the customer it was meant for.

The law itself does not move when the tools do. Whatever the systems can do next year, generating new business will still need the reach and the magnetism, so the question you carry into the section is which of the two this company is short of, and every recommendation you write gets sized to that answer.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 4 section

The heading is `PRINCIPLE 4: Active & Passive`.

Two to five short paragraphs. This is usually the longest section in the document, and the reference gives it four plus a bulleted list plus a full-page diagram. Length here is earned rather than padded: this law tends to produce more because the intake tends to produce more on it, and because the recommendations are concrete enough to fill space honestly.

Build it from the four moves: the observation, the law applied, the opportunity, the honest gap. Open on what the owner said about how they are currently getting customers. Their words, close to verbatim. If they told you they are scaling up outbound, open on that. If they told you referrals are the whole pipeline, open on that.

Name the half of the law they are already on before proposing the other half. This is what keeps the section from reading as a lecture. The owner is already doing something. Say what it is, credit it, then say which side is carrying nothing.

Use an italic bulleted list when you name a menu of passive assets. The list is a menu rather than a plan, so keep it short, italicize it, and let it end open. It carries options, not the argument.

Propose the full-page marketing garden diagram to the orchestrator when this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement, which it often does. Only one or two diagrams go into any assessment, so you propose and the orchestrator decides. The diagram is a header card naming the asset that already exists, which in the reference is the one-page contact form, a one-line statement of the law in the owner's own terms, the six labelled cards, and a footer line tying it back to the tools.

Vocabulary this law owns: active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil, reach and magnetism, planting, tending, the weak side, the loop.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| Law | Its images |
|---|---|
| 1. Intention and Action | the mirror, the drill and the hole, intention before action, the conversation |
| 2. Big and Small | the snowflake and the glacier, translation, the pattern, figurative scale |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 5. Wins and Losses | hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the continuous scale |
| 6. Rhythm and Fit | rhythm, the swing, the kite, the wind, the tide, the arc, fit |
| 7. Karma and Value | the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf, giving and extracting, the bay, the net |

That is the whole table minus Law 4's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

You may name another law when a finding hands off to it, in one clause, without arguing that finding again in your own imagery.

**The garden is a season and the recommendation has to start this month.** Those two rules collide in this section and nowhere else, so settle it here rather than leaving the writer to notice it mid-paragraph. A garden is a season-long build. Every recommendation in this system has to be startable within a month. The resolution is to name the first asset as the month's move, and then say plainly that the rest of it is a season. Do not compress a season into four weeks on paper, and do not push the whole thing out past the month rule. When the owner has no capacity to start at all, because another law's finding is standing in the way, use `blocked_on` and name that law. A garden recommended to an owner who is the bottleneck on every deliverable will be agreed with and not acted on.

## Worked example

The reference assessment in `references/reference_assessment.md` is the target. Verity Site Works is a nine-person commercial land surveying firm in Reno, invented for teaching, and its PRINCIPLE 4 section reads in full:

> All of your new work comes from referrals and from you calling engineers you know. I would not touch that. You are not sending bulk mail to a bought list, you are calling people who have a reason to take the call, and that is active marketing done the way it is supposed to be done. It is also all of it.
>
> When I asked whether new customers would still find you if you stopped working for a month, you said the phone would ring for maybe two weeks off relationships, then nothing. Two weeks is a real measure of how good those relationships are. It is also a measure of how little is running while you are not. The half of this principle that works whether or not you are on the phone is currently a one-page contact form.
>
> Everything you would put in the open, you already own. Assets worth considering:
>
> - *A short write-up of one job you did for a repeat client, once you can say why it went well,*
> - *A one-page guide to what changes in a county scope, and when it changes,*
> - *Your own turnaround times, if the record is there to pull,*
> - *A monthly note to the engineers and developers you already call,*
> - *Two minutes on the question you get asked most in a first call,*
> - *Etc.*
>
> None of that pays this month, which is why it has not happened in eleven years. Start one piece of it anyway, and only one, because you are already the bottleneck on every deliverable and a six-asset project is not something this firm has room for until Principle 2 moves. Start with the county scope guide. You already know that material cold and it is the thing your customers ask about most.

What it is doing, line by line:

- It opens on how the owner is actually getting customers today, in the owner's own terms. Referrals, and Dana calling engineers Dana already knows. The observation belongs to the intake rather than to the framework.
- It credits the half that is already running, and credits it specifically. Not bulk mail to a bought list, but calls to people who have a reason to take the call. Naming what is good about it is what keeps the section from reading as a lecture.
- "It is also all of it." One short sentence turns the credit into the finding without taking the credit back.
- The month-off diagnostic is in the section with the answer verbatim. Two weeks off relationships, then nothing. The measure is the owner's, not the assessor's.
- It reads those two weeks both ways. As evidence the relationships are real, and as a measure of how little runs while the owner is not working. One fact carries the praise and the problem.
- It names the passive side by what actually exists. A one-page contact form. No adjective on it and no complaint about it, just the size of the thing.
- The asset menu is italic, one line each, and it ends in *Etc.* It is a menu to pick from rather than a plan handed over, and the *Etc.* keeps it open.
- Every asset on the menu is made of something the firm already owns: a job already delivered, county scopes already known cold, turnaround times already sitting in the record, the question already asked in every first call.
- The recommendation is one asset rather than six, and the section says why in the same sentence. The owner is already the bottleneck on every deliverable, so the garden waits on Principle 2. A correct recommendation the owner has no room to start is the wrong place to begin.
- It picks the first asset and gives the reason for that pick, then stops. Material the owner knows cold, and the thing customers ask about most. That is the month's move, and the rest of the garden is a season.

## What you hand back

The five-part block from the chapter-skill contract:

1. `verdict`: two verdicts, not one, because this law has two sides and a single word hides which one is broken. Return one verdict per side, each drawn from `strong`, `working`, `thin`, `not observed`, written as `thin (passive) / working (active)`. A company can be strong on one side and absent on the other. The orchestrator ranks this law on the weaker of the two.
2. `section`: the prose for the full assessment, ready to sit under the `PRINCIPLE 4: Active & Passive` heading. Italic bullets where the reference would use them.
3. `quick_read`: two or three sentences, one observation and one opportunity, written fresh rather than cut down from the section.
4. `opportunities`: the concrete moves you named, listed so the orchestrator can rank them and Principle 7 can walk them in order.
5. `gaps`: what you could not assess, with the questions the owner should answer. These go in the document.

Optionally `blocked_on`. The law whose finding has to move first, when this law's recommendation is right but cannot be started until something else changes. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Optionally `diagram`, when this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement. Propose it with the six slots already filled from the intake, so the orchestrator can judge it on the contents rather than on the idea.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

## Self-check

Run this before handing the block back.

- No AI product name, company name, or version number appears anywhere.
- The weak side was diagnosed before anything was recommended. The month-off question was asked and its answer is in the record.
- The section names the half of the law the owner is already on before proposing the other half.
- The garden assets were chosen from what this company actually knows and already has, and any slot with nothing behind it was left empty and called out.
- Only Law 4's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
- Any comparison to an outside industry is a real, checkable practice, used to make one narrow point.
- Every claim traces to something marked `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` in the intake. Anything else is cut or turned into a question.
- No invented person, client, number, statistic, or anecdote.
- Every recommendation can be started this month by a company this size.
- Any menu of assets is italic, short, and ends open. It carries options rather than the argument.
- It does not end on a manufactured takeaway. It ends on the content, a question, or what happens next.
- First and second person throughout. "I" and "you". No "the client", no "stakeholders".
- The verdict matches the section and carries both sides, one per side, in the `thin (passive) / working (active)` form.
