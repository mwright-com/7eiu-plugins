## The law

Effort without timing is wasted energy, so feel the rhythm first and then act.

The book's image is a swing. Walk up to a child on a swing and shove whenever you feel like it and you will fight the swing, get kicked, and tire yourself out, and the child goes nowhere. The parent who knows what they are doing does not push harder. They push at the top of the arc, a small touch in time with the swing's own rhythm, and the child climbs on almost no effort. The parable carries the same thing forward: Lira runs herself empty dragging a kite along the ground, then waits, feels the wind gather, takes three light steps, and the kite leaps off her hands.

This law governs relations. Where the company sits against a market that keeps moving, and whether its effort is feeding that rhythm or fighting it.

## What this law is looking for in a business

Signals the law is being obeyed:

- The owner can name something that is moving in the market now and was not moving two years ago, and say how they found out.
- The owner can name who else is going after the same customer and what those competitors say to win the work.
- The owner can say what a customer would pay them a premium for, in the customer's terms rather than the company's.
- Somebody can point to a push that produced almost nothing and a light touch that produced something, and the two have been compared.
- The company takes several small swings a season rather than betting the year on one.
- There is a standing habit of watching the market, with a person's name on it and a place the findings go.
- Work was started during the last good stretch that was meant for the turn after it.
- The offer has been changed at least once because the market moved, not because a customer complained.
- Somebody is watching what competitors are automating away, and asking what that leaves open.

Signals the law is being broken:

- Brutal effort against flat results, and the plan for next quarter is more of it.
- Pushing harder on something whose season has passed, and calling that persistence.
- Staying cautious through the one upswing that would have carried the company, because the last downswing is still in the room.
- Nobody can say what is moving in the market that was not moving two years ago.
- The company found fit once and stopped watching, so yesterday's fit is drifting into today's mismatch.
- Nobody is preparing during the good season for the turn.
- Competitors are described by name and size and nothing else, with no read on what they are actually selling.
- Every attempt is large, slow, and expensive, so the company gets two swings a year.
- The question "is this working" gets answered with how hard everyone is trying.
- Market information arrives from customers who are already leaving.

## What to ask

From Round 7 of the intake interview:

- What is moving in your market right now that was not moving two years ago?
- Who else is going after the same customer, and what is their pitch?
- Where do you fit against that, and what would a customer pay you a premium for?
- Where have you pushed hard and gotten nothing back? Where have you barely pushed and something happened anyway?
- What are you watching, and how do you find out when something shifts?

Probes to add:

- Walk me through the last three things you tried to grow this. For each one, what did you expect, and what actually happened? This is the question that separates persistence from stubbornness. Not how hard are you trying, but is anything responding. Ask for the response, not the effort.
- How long does one attempt take you, start to finish, before you know whether it worked? The answer tells you how many swings a season this company gets.
- What are your competitors taking a person out of right now, and what happens to the customer when they do?
- When the last good stretch ended, what did you wish you had built while it was still running?
- If the thing you are pushing hardest on stopped working tomorrow, how long before you noticed?
- What did you try that nobody here mentions anymore, and what happened to it?

Mark every answer `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]`, and mark what you asked and did not get as `[gap]`. A read of a competitor's website is `[observed]` about the website and nothing more. What the market is doing is not something you may infer and then write as a claim.

## Opportunity patterns

**1. Reading the flickers honestly.**
Missing when the owner can describe the effort in detail and cannot describe the response at all.

- One-person shop: lay out the last six months on one page. What you tried, when, and what actually happened after. Then ask, of each line, whether anything responded, separate from what you want to be true. A tool is useful here because it has no stake in the answer.
- Team of ten: do it with two people in the room and the numbers on the table, once a quarter. The rule is that hope and fear both stay out of the reading. Somebody writes down what responded and what did not.
- Scaling or acquiring: run it per line of business and per acquired site. A company can be riding a rising rhythm in one place and shoving dead air in another, and the average hides both.

**2. More swings per season.**
Missing when every attempt is a heavy shove and there are only two of them a year.

- One-person shop: cut the size of an attempt until you can run four in the time one used to take. Half-built is fine when the point is to feel whether anything catches.
- Team of ten: pick one thing you were going to do big next quarter and run three small versions of it instead, each with a different angle at the same customer. Decide in advance what a response looks like.
- Scaling or acquiring: use the sites you already have as the test bed. Three markets running three versions gives you a read in a month that a single rollout would take a year to give you.

**3. A standing watch on the market.**
Missing when the owner finds out something shifted from a customer who has already gone.

- One-person shop: one hour, same time every week. Read what your competitors published, what your customers are asking about, and what changed in the rules you work under. Write three lines. A tool can do the gathering so the hour is spent reading rather than hunting.
- Team of ten: give the hour a name on it and a place the three lines go, so the whole team sees the same market. Once a quarter, ask what in those notes has been true for three months running.
- Scaling or acquiring: this is diligence you run continuously rather than at the deal. A watch that has been running a year tells you which targets are riding a rhythm and which are shoving.

**4. Building during the upswing for the turn.**
Missing when the good season is being spent entirely on the good season.

- One-person shop: while the work is flowing, spend one day a month on the thing that will matter when it stops. Usually that is a second kind of customer, or the written version of what only you can do.
- Team of ten: name one project that only exists because the turn is coming, fund it while you have money, and do not let it get eaten by this quarter's demand.
- Scaling or acquiring: the upswing is when your balance sheet is strongest and the sellers are most expensive. Decide now what you would buy in a downswing, and at what price, while nobody is panicking.

**5. Naming the premium on a person.**
Missing when the company has a human element the market values and describes it as a cost.

- One-person shop: write the sentence a customer would say about why they call you rather than the cheaper option. Then put it where a stranger can read it.
- Team of ten: find where the human element actually shows up in the work, protect the time it takes, and price it. If competitors are removing it, say so in your own words to your own market.
- Scaling or acquiring: make it a standard the acquired sites have to meet, and measure it. It is a position, and a position you cannot see is a position you will cut in the first bad quarter.

**6. Openings made by competitors who bet wrong.**
Missing when the owner watches competitors for pricing and nothing else.

- One-person shop: keep a short list of the operators in your market who removed the person entirely, and check on them twice a year. Some of their customers will be looking.
- Team of ten: track the individual operators building momentum in your market as well as the big names. They are the ones who eventually want a back office, and they are cheaper to talk to early. The read on who they are and what their arrival means belongs to this law; if the move is to recruit them, that is a channel decision and Principle 4 makes it.
- Scaling or acquiring: a competitor who bet everything on the wrong rhythm is an acquisition target with a customer list attached. Know which ones you would want before their season ends.

Every recommendation you write has to be startable inside a month. If it needs a hire, a budget cycle, or a system nobody has bought yet, shrink it until it starts on a Tuesday.

## The AI angle

A rhythm is a pattern over time, and finding patterns over time is what these systems do best. That is the whole case for using them here. The market's motion is spread across months of small facts that no busy owner has time to hold at once, and a tool will hold all of it and tell you what it sees.

The second use is harder and more valuable. A machine has neither hope nor fear. An owner in the middle of a hard year has both, and both distort the reading: hope inventing flickers that are not there, fear erasing ones that are. You can lay the actual record in front of a tool, what was tried and what happened, and ask whether anything is responding. It will not tell you to keep going because it admires your grit, and it will not tell you to quit because it is tired.

The third use is speed. These tools collapse the time between attempts, so what used to be a month of building and testing one idea can be a week of trying several. That is more swings per season, and more swings means more chances to feel the rhythm before the money runs out.

The fourth use is the one companies skip. The tools do not get drunk on a good season. At the height of an upswing, when everyone in the building is certain it will last, you can still use them to ask what the downswing looks like and what should be built now, while there is momentum and money to build it with.

Never name an AI product, company, or version number. Write "AI tools", "these types of solutions", "a tool that can". A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

## The premium on the human touch

This law is where the question of what a market will still pay a person for gets decided, so handle it here and not in another section.

The benchmark surfaced this as a finding about one market, and it stays a finding about one market. Write it when the intake hands it to you. When the owner tells you that competitors in their market are taking the person out of the customer-facing part, name what that leaves open for a company that keeps one: it can do the work efficiently and still put a person on the other end, on ground the others are walking away from. If nothing in the intake says the competition is automating the person away, there is no finding here to write.

Write it as a positioning finding, not a sentimental one. The claim is not that people are nicer than machines. The claim is that a scarce thing gets priced higher, and the company holding it should know it is holding it. That means naming it, protecting the time it takes, and saying it out loud to the market rather than treating it as overhead that survived.

The same reading points at two openings. Competitors who bet everything on removing the person often end up with a customer base that is looking, which makes them acquisition targets rather than threats. And individual operators building momentum in the same market are worth watching for the same reason, because a person with customers and no back office eventually wants one. That market read belongs to this law: who is entering, what their arrival means, and which of them becomes an acquisition target. It passes to Law 4 only when the recommendation is to recruit them, because recruiting is a channel decision and gets argued there. Write the read here, and leave the recruiting to Principle 4 so the same finding does not reach the owner twice under two names.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 6 section

Heading: `PRINCIPLE 6: Rhythm & Fit`

Length: two to four short paragraphs. This section is usually one of the fuller ones, because the owner has spent years reading their own market and will have handed you a lot in Round 7.

Use the four moves from the benchmark spec, in whatever order the material wants: the observation, the law applied, the opportunity, the honest gap.

Open by handing the market knowledge back to the owner. They know their market and you do not, and saying so is what buys you the right to add anything. Then add only what you can actually add.

If the company has fit, open on the evidence of it, which is usually a small push that produced a large response. If the company does not have fit yet, open on the swings: how many they get a season, and how they are reading what comes back. If you genuinely could not tell from the intake, say that and write the section as a set of questions. `not observed` is an honest verdict and the framing paragraph can use it.

This is the section where an outside reference is most likely to earn its place, because the assessor may know something about where the market is moving that the owner does not. If you cite something, it has to be real and findable, with a link, and used to make one narrow point. Hedge what you cannot verify, the way the benchmark does with "at least that's how they seem to present themselves". Never invent a source, a company, a founder, or a statistic. If you do not have a real one, make the point without it. A section with no citation is fine. A section with a fabricated one does not ship.

Vocabulary this law owns: rhythm, the swing, the arc, the kite, the wind, the tide, fit, the flickers, feeding the rhythm rather than fighting it. Use one image with weight rather than all of them lightly.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| 2. Big and Small | the snowflake and the glacier, translation, the pattern, figurative scale |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 4. Active and Passive Marketing | active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil |
| 5. Wins and Losses | hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the continuous scale |
| 7. Karma and Value | the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf, giving and extracting, the bay, the net |

That is the whole table minus Law 6's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

You may name another law when a finding hands off to it. You may not argue that finding again in your own imagery.

First person and second person. "I", "you". Hedge to what you actually learned. Short paragraphs. End on the content, on what happens next, or on a question.

## Worked example

The benchmark's PRINCIPLE 6 section read in full:

> For the sixth principle, this is where the meat of all of your past research lies, as well as in your intention to expand. What is going on with the market now, and where does Clear RCM fit in terms of AI is something I may be able to add to.
>
> I discovered a pure AI play in Lassie AI. At least that's how they seem to present themselves. In the Andreesen-Horowitz interview with founders Steijn Pelle and Frédéric Renken (@a16z - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fpg_8aiBxx4 ) they spoke about the need to first act as a black box - to manually do the work - THEN automate.
>
> To your point, however, there is a level of soft touch that is worth the premium versus an AI-only solution. Although the healthcare industry is trying its best to automate customer handling, the company that can do this efficiently and still provide a human voice will have a clear advantage. The market is clearly there. Again, leveraging AI to monitor and evaluate trends like this is where you may see the most value. It may also lead to acquisition opportunities from failing AI-centric companies, as well as with individual operators as they build momentum.

One note on the quote above. The firm is Andreessen Horowitz; the source document's spelling is reproduced here exactly as written, so do not carry it into a new document.

What it is doing:

- The first sentence gives the market knowledge back to the owner. The research is his, the expansion plan is his, and the assessor says so before adding anything.
- "is something I may be able to add to" is the whole posture of the section in nine words. It offers a contribution, not a correction.
- The outside reference is real. A named company, two named founders, a named interview, and a working link, all findable by the reader in a minute.
- "At least that's how they seem to present themselves" is the hedge that keeps the claim honest. The assessor read their positioning and says exactly that, rather than asserting what the company is.
- The reference is used for one narrow point and then dropped. Do the work manually first, then automate. It is not a survey of the market and it does not carry the argument alone.
- "To your point, however" builds on something the owner already said. The premium on the soft touch was his observation, and the section extends it rather than introducing it as news.
- The positioning claim is stated flat, with no sentiment attached. An industry automating customer handling leaves an opening for whoever can be efficient and still answer the phone.
- The AI recommendation is described only by what it does, monitoring and evaluating trends, with no product named anywhere.
- The closing sentence turns a market observation into two concrete openings, failing AI-centric companies and individual operators gaining momentum, and it ends there rather than on a summary line.

Now an illustration. **The company below is fictional, invented for this file, and must never be presented to a client as a real engagement.**

Meridian Facility Care, an eleven-person janitorial services company bidding on commercial office contracts. Verdict: `working`.

> You know this market better than I do, so take the reading and tell me where I have it wrong. What you described sounds like a company that has fit. Three of your last four contracts came from a property manager who had already worked with you somewhere else, and you told me you barely pushed for any of them. That is the light touch that catches, and it is the clearest sign in the whole intake that your offer is in time with something real.
>
> What I did not hear was anyone watching the rhythm. When I asked what is moving in your market that was not moving two years ago, the answer was that the bids got tighter, and that was it. Meanwhile you mentioned that two of the larger regionals now route every service request through an app with no phone number on it, and that your crews get called directly by tenants who have that number saved. You said that like it was a scheduling problem. It may be your position. If the rest of the market is taking the person out of the customer-facing part, being the company a building manager can actually reach is worth something, and it is worth naming in a bid rather than leaving it to be discovered.
>
> There may be an opportunity in a standing hour. One hour a week, same time, where somebody reads what the regionals published, what came out of the last three bid losses, and what the property managers are asking about. Three lines written down, in one place, every week. AI tools are good at the gathering part, which is what makes the hour readable instead of a research project. By the third month you would have something the bid narrative can be built on, and you would find out about a shift from your own notes rather than from a manager who has already signed elsewhere.

## What you hand back

Return the five-part block from the chapter-skill contract. Add `diagram` only if this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement.

Add `blocked_on` when the recommendation here is right but cannot be started until something else changes. It names the law whose finding has to move first. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

Filled in for the fictional janitorial illustration:

**verdict**
`working`

**section**
The three paragraphs above, ready to drop under `PRINCIPLE 6: Rhythm & Fit`.

**quick_read**
Three of your last four contracts came to you through a property manager you barely chased, which is what fit looks like. Nobody here is watching what the market does next, and the reachable-human position your crews already hold is being treated as a scheduling problem instead of a reason to win a bid.

**opportunities**
1. One standing hour a week watching competitors, bid outcomes, and what property managers are asking, with three lines written down each time.
2. Name the reachable-human position in the bid narrative and price it, rather than leaving it to be discovered after the contract starts.
3. Run three small versions of the next bid approach instead of one large one, and decide in advance what a response looks like.

**gaps**
I did not get a clear read on why the four bids you lost last year went the way they did, or whether the losses have anything in common.

- *What did the winner offer on each of those four, as far as you could tell?*
- *Which of your current contracts would be hardest to replace, and how would you know it was slipping?*

## Self-check

Before handing the block back, walk this list.

1. No AI product name, company name, or version number in the section, the quick read, or the opportunities. The one narrow exception is naming a real competitor or market participant the assessor actually found, with a real source, because that is a market fact rather than a tool recommendation.
2. Any cited source is real, findable, and linked, and it is used to make one narrow point rather than to carry the argument.
3. No invented founders, companies, statistics, or interviews. If the reference is not real, the point gets made without it.
4. The owner's own market knowledge is credited before anything is added, and anything added is offered as an addition.
5. Only Law 6's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
6. The persistence-versus-stubbornness question is answered with evidence of what responded, not with encouragement to keep going or a suggestion to quit.
7. Every claim about the market traces to a `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` entry, and everything else became a question in `gaps`.
8. Any claim you cannot verify carries a hedge in the sentence, the way "at least that's how they seem to present themselves" does.
9. If the human-touch premium appears, it is written as positioning with a reason attached, and not as sentiment.
10. Every recommendation can be started within a month, without a hire or a budget cycle.
11. The section is as long as the material and no longer, two to four paragraphs, with nothing padded to match another section.
12. The last sentence ends on content, a next step, or a question, and not on a manufactured takeaway.
