# The reference assessment

**Everything below is invented.** Verity Site Works does not exist. Dana, Rafa, Tomasz,
Priya and Cordero + Ash are not real people or companies, and nothing described here
happened. It is written to the standard a real assessment has to hit, so the team has
something complete to write against.

Read it before you write your first section. The spec in `benchmark_spec.md` describes
this document's shape; this is the document itself.

## The assessment

Looking at Verity Site Works from the outside with my 7 EIU framework, the first thing I
want to correct is my own first read. You are clear about what you want, and the firm holds
something real, which is the fast turnaround and the picked-up phone that engineers tell you
about constantly. But the three principles that govern how a company reaches and keeps
people outside its own walls all came back thin. There is nothing in the open for a stranger
to find, nobody can say why your best customer keeps coming back, and two new firms are
advertising something you do not do while nobody here is watching them. The leverage sits in
getting what only you and Rafa know onto paper, because that is what the second crew is
waiting on.

![7 Principles Diagram](figures/seven_principles.png)

What the firm brings that is not the product is the thing engineers keep telling you about.
You turn work around fast and you answer the phone yourself. In a trade where the standard
complaint is a four-week lead time and a voicemail box, that is not a small asset, and it is
the one thing a competitor with better equipment cannot buy outright.

Assuming you already know that, the next step is to run the second crew through the seven
principles.

**PRINCIPLE 1: Intent & Action**

"I want to get to the point where I'm not the bottleneck on every deliverable, and I'd like
to add a second crew without the quality going sideways." You said that without a run-up,
and the two halves are one sentence rather than two goals. A second crew is not possible
while every drawing waits on you, so the first half is the condition for the second. That is
more clarity than I usually find, and it is worth saying plainly before I say anything else.

The clarity stops with you, though. Nobody I heard about has been asked to say the goal
back. You stamp everything, and you told me why: "I stamp it, it's my license." I am not
going to argue with that. But the stamp is one decision, and there are others on your desk
that nobody has been told they can make without you. Tomasz is the clearest case. He has
been using AI tools on his own for drafting cleanup and field notes, nobody asked him to,
and you have not looked at it. Whatever he built is either how the firm does that work or it
is going away, and right now it is neither.

Two I did not get far enough into:

- *When a crew is on site and what they find does not match the record, who decides what
  happens next, and does that decision wait for you?*
- *What has Rafa been telling new techs that has never been written down?*

**PRINCIPLE 2: Big & Small**

Nothing about how a job gets set up, run, or checked exists anywhere except in your head and
Rafa's. That is the finding the rest of this document keeps running into.

Larger firms in your trade are not better surveyors. They have written procedure, which is
what lets them put a crew in the field without the principal riding along. That is a pattern
you can shrink. Not their org chart, not their software budget. One page: how a job gets set
up, what the crew checks before leaving site, what gets verified before it reaches you.
Write it from the next three jobs as they happen rather than from memory, because memory
produces the version you wish were true.

Start with Tomasz. He has already built something for drafting cleanup and field notes, and
looking at it costs you an hour. If it is good, it is the first piece of written method you
have and it came free. If it is not, you have learned something about where he needs
guidance. Either way the hour is worth more than the hour.

**PRINCIPLE 3: Be Consistent**

Fast turnaround and a picked-up phone. That is the note, and engineers tell you about it
constantly, which is how you know it is real rather than something you believe about
yourself.

It is held by two people's memory. That is fine at nine people and it breaks at fourteen,
which is where a second crew puts you. The note will not survive by being cared about; it
survives by being written down, which is Principle 2's work. Keep doing what you're doing,
and understand that the thing keeping it consistent right now is that you personally touch
every deliverable, which is the same fact you told me you want to stop being true.

**PRINCIPLE 4: Active & Passive**

All of your new work comes from referrals and from you calling engineers you know. I would
not touch that. You are not sending bulk mail to a bought list, you are calling people who
have a reason to take the call, and that is active marketing done the way it is supposed to
be done. It is also all of it.

When I asked whether new customers would still find you if you stopped working for a month,
you said the phone would ring for maybe two weeks off relationships, then nothing. Two weeks
is a real measure of how good those relationships are. It is also a measure of how little is
running while you are not. The half of this principle that works whether or not you are on
the phone is currently a one-page contact form.

Everything you would put in the open, you already own. Assets worth considering:

- *A short write-up of one job you did for a repeat client, once you can say why it went well,*
- *A one-page guide to what changes in a county scope, and when it changes,*
- *Your own turnaround times, if the record is there to pull,*
- *A monthly note to the engineers and developers you already call,*
- *Two minutes on the question you get asked most in a first call,*
- *Etc.*

None of that pays this month, which is why it has not happened in eleven years. Start one
piece of it anyway, and only one, because you are already the bottleneck on every deliverable
and a six-asset project is not something this firm has room for until Principle 2 moves.
Start with the county scope guide. You already know that material cold and it is the thing
your customers ask about most.

![The Marketing Garden](figures/marketing_garden.png)

**PRINCIPLE 5: Winning & Losing**

You named Cordero + Ash before I finished asking. Repeat work, and they refer others. When I
asked why, the answer was "we get along." I believe that. It is also the warmest relationship
in the business, and nobody in the building can say what makes it warm, which means it cannot
be run again on purpose with the next firm.

The cold end you can already feel. County work, slow to pay, scopes changing constantly. None
of it is written anywhere, so it lives in the firm as a feeling about county jobs rather than
a list of what changes and what each change costs you. A list you can act on.

Then there is the mixed-use bid. You never found out why you lost it, and you told me you
assumed price. I do not know whether it was price, and neither do you. There is nothing
stopping you from calling whoever ran that selection and asking.

What I did not learn:

- *Which customers are the success stories, and why?*
- *Which have been difficult, and why?*
- *Which would switch at the first opportunity?*
- *Was the mixed-use job actually lost on price, and have you asked?*

**PRINCIPLE 6: Rhythm & Fit**

Two new firms in two years, both advertising drone and lidar capture, and you do not do drone
work. Nobody at Verity is watching them.

I am not going to tell you to buy a drone. I do not know your trade well enough to say
whether that capture method suits the work you take, and you do. What I can say is that two
competitors moving the same direction inside two years is a rhythm, and right now you will
find out where it went from a bid sheet rather than from watching. The cost of watching is
close to nothing.

The harder question underneath: what does your market pay a person for, as against what it
pays equipment for? Your differentiator is speed and a picked-up phone, both of which are
human. If the trade is moving toward capture-as-commodity, the human half is where your
premium lives, and that is worth knowing deliberately rather than discovering.

**PRINCIPLE 7: Karma & Value**

When I asked where you would be tempted to take a shortcut, you said "No. I stamp it, it's my
license. That's the whole business." That settles the ledger question for a firm like this
one, so I will spend the rest of this section on the other six.

The previous 6 principles suggest the following:

Say the outcome you gave me out loud to Rafa, Tomasz and Priya, and find out whether it comes
back the same way. Get what is in your head and Rafa's onto paper, starting with how a job
gets set up, run and checked, and look at what Tomasz has already built before you build
anything next to it. Hold the fast turnaround and the picked-up phone, and understand that
right now it is held by you personally touching everything. Put one thing in the open besides
the contact form, so the two weeks of ringing phone you described is not the whole of what
happens without you. Find out why Cordero + Ash keeps coming back, and make the call on the
mixed-use job you lost. Then start watching the two firms advertising drone and lidar, and
decide what it means rather than finding out from a bid sheet. All of it is startable this
month, and the tools carry a real share of the labor on every piece once you have decided
what you are actually making. I believe that gets you to the point where you are "not the
bottleneck on every deliverable," and to a second crew "without the quality going sideways,"
which is how you put it to me.

## What this document is doing, move by move

The spec covers the shape. These are the moves worth copying.

**It takes a position in the first two sentences.** The framing paragraph does not summarize
findings. It says where the assessor stands, corrects its own earlier read, and points at one
place.

**Section lengths are uneven, on purpose.** Principle 3 is two paragraphs. Principle 4 is
five plus a figure. That reflects what was actually learned about each, and padding the thin
ones is the most common way one of these goes wrong.

**Every claim traces to the intake.** "You said that without a run-up." "You told me you
assumed price." Where the assessor did not learn enough, the section says so and hands the
questions back in italics rather than filling the hole with a guess.

**It declines to claim expertise it does not have.** Principle 6: "I am not going to tell you
to buy a drone. I do not know your trade well enough to say." The reader is the expert on
their own trade, and pretending otherwise costs the whole document.

**A recommendation that is blocked says so.** Principle 4 recommends one asset rather than
six, and names why: the owner is already the bottleneck, so the garden waits on Principle 2.
A correct recommendation the owner cannot start is not the place to begin.

**Principle 7 introduces nothing new.** It walks the six earlier moves in law order and
closes on the owner's own words for their own goal.

**No AI product is named anywhere.** The document says "AI tools" and what a capability does.
