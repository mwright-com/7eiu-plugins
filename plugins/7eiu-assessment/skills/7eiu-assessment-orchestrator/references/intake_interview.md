# The intake interview

The assessment is only as good as this conversation. Everything in the finished document
has to trace back to something said here or to a file the owner handed over. There is no
research step that can substitute for it.

Run it as a conversation, not a form. Ask in rounds. React to what you hear. Follow a
thread when it opens rather than marching to the next bullet.

---

## How to pace it

**Round 0, the frame.** Two minutes. Tell them what this is: seven questions' worth of
conversation about how their business actually runs, which turns into a written read of
where their openings are. Ask permission to be direct, and tell them the honest gaps get
written down as gaps.

**Round 1, the company.** Get the facts you cannot write without.

**Rounds 2 through 8, one per law.** Three to six questions each. Ask the opening
question, listen, then follow with the probes that the answer actually earns. Skip probes
you already have answers to.

**Round 9, the close.** Their words for their own goal, and anything they want looked at
that you did not ask about.

Between rounds, reflect one thing back in their own words and check it. That check is what
lets you write the sentence later without hedging it into mush.

**When the owner is short on time**, run Round 1, then one opening question per law, and
produce the Quick Read only. Say plainly that the full assessment needs the longer
conversation.

---

## Round 1: the company

- What is the business, in the way you would say it to someone at a barbecue?
- Who pays you, and what were they doing before they found you?
- How many people, and who does what? Get first names and what each person is good at.
- How long have you been at it, and what changed most recently?
- What are you trying to do in the next twelve months? Use their words and write them
  down verbatim. This sentence anchors the whole document.
- Is anything already in motion toward that? A hire, a campaign, a product, a deal?
- Who is this addressed to, and where does the company sit? Name, title, company and
  mailing address. The cover page needs all four.

Also ask, once: **what do you have that is not the product?** The benchmark's
differentiator paragraph comes from this question. In the Clear RCM engagement the answer
was a team that cares about each other, not only about clients.

---

## Round 2: Law 1, Intention and Action (Leadership)

- When something needs deciding, how does it get decided, and by whom?
- What is the one outcome that matters most right now? Say it in one sentence.
- Where does the team get instructions that are clear, and where do they get instructions
  that are roughly right and have to be interpreted?
- When you or your team use AI tools, what do you typically ask for? Get an actual example.
- What context about this business would an outsider never guess?

## Round 3: Law 2, Big and Small (Process)

- What do the biggest companies in your line of work do that you cannot?
- What do you do that they cannot, or no longer do?
- Which parts of the work are the same every time, and which need a person each time?
- Who on the team already uses AI tools, and for what? Who does not, and why?
- Where does the work bottleneck when volume goes up?

## Round 4: Law 3, Being Consistent (Operations)

- What should a customer be able to count on from you, every single time?
- Where does that slip when you get busy, and what breaks first?
- Does your work sound like you across every channel, or does it change depending on who
  wrote it?
- What is written down, and what lives only in somebody's head?
- Who is the longest-tenured person here, and what do they hold that nobody else does?

## Round 5: Law 4, Active and Passive Marketing (Marketing)

- How does a new customer find you today? Walk through the last three.
- **If you stopped working for a month, would new customers still find you?** This is the
  diagnostic question. Everything else in this round follows from the answer.
- What are you doing to reach out right now, and how is it going?
- What do you have sitting in the open that a stranger can find and use? Site, guides,
  case studies, videos, a newsletter, anything.
(New entrants and who else is chasing the same customer belong to Round 7. Law 6 owns the
market read. Ask it once, there.)

## Round 6: Law 5, Wins and Losses (Support)

- Who are your success stories, and why did those work?
- Which customers have been difficult, and why?
- Are there customers who would switch at the first opportunity? Do you know which?
- When you lose one, what happens? Does anyone write down why?
- When something works well, does anyone figure out why it worked?
- Where do customer stories live right now, if anywhere?

## Round 7: Law 6, Rhythm and Fit (Relations)

- What is moving in your market right now that was not moving two years ago?
- Who else is going after the same customer, and what is their pitch?
- Where do you fit against that, and what would a customer pay you a premium for?
- Where have you pushed hard and gotten nothing back? Where have you barely pushed and
  something happened anyway?
- What are you watching, and how do you find out when something shifts?

## Round 8: Law 7, Karma and Value (Evaluation)

- Would you be comfortable if a customer could see everything you do and why?
- Where does the money actually come from, and what does the customer get for it?
- Where are you tempted to take a shortcut right now?
- What would people say about you in this market if you were not in the room?
- What do you want to still be true about this business in ten years?

---

## Round 9: the close

- What did I not ask about that you think matters?
- Say the twelve-month goal one more time, in your words.
- Anything you want to hand me? Site, deck, numbers, notes, recordings.

---

## Recording what you hear

Keep a running intake record with one entry per fact, and mark the source of each:

- `[said]`: the owner or a team member said it in the interview.
- `[doc]`: it came out of a file they supplied. Name the file.
- `[observed]`: you saw it yourself in something they showed you.
- `[gap]`: you asked and did not get an answer, or did not ask.

**Only `[said]`, `[doc]` and `[observed]` may become a claim in the document.** Every
`[gap]` is either left out or written into the document as a gap, with the questions the
owner should answer. That second option is the move that made the benchmark credible, so
use it rather than hiding the hole.

Nothing else may be added. Not a plausible inference about their staff, not an industry
statistic, not a comparison to a company you were not told about. If a section feels thin,
the honest fix is a shorter section, or a question.
