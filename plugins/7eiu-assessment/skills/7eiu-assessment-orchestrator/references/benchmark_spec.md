# The benchmark: what a finished 7EIU assessment looks like

Every skill in this team writes against one reference document: the post-mortem
assessment Maurice Wright delivered to Clear RCM after an AI-readiness engagement.
That document is the target. If a section you write would not sit comfortably beside
it, the section is wrong.

This file describes the benchmark so the team can reproduce it without the original
in hand.

---

## 1. What the document actually is

An outside read of a real company, organized by the seven laws, written by one person
to one person. It is not a report. It is a letter with headings.

It is eight pages. Roughly 1,800 words of prose plus two full-page diagrams. Every
observation in it came out of a working session and a site visit. Nothing in it was
researched from the outside and dressed up as insight.

The tone: a person who spent time inside the business telling the owner what he saw,
including the parts he did not get to see.

---

## 2. Page-by-page anatomy

**Cover.** Running header with the author's name and city on the left, email and phone
on the right. Mark above the title. A small-caps kicker (`POST MORTEM`). The title is
the client name plus the engagement (`Clear RCM - AI Readiness Training`). A one-line
subtitle in grey: `An evaluation using the 7 EIU principles`. At the foot, two columns:
`PREPARED FOR` (name, title, company, street address) and `PREPARED BY` (name, company,
city, email, phone).

**The framing paragraph.** Three to five sentences. It states the vantage point, says
which laws the company already satisfies, and names where the leverage is. From the
benchmark:

> Looking at Clear RCM from an outside perspective with my 7 EIU framework it seems
> that by virtue of the company being worth acquiring in the first place, the first 6
> principles are already in place. As the acquirer, the 7th principle, "Karma & Value"
> is where you begin to level up.

Note what it does. It does not open with a summary of findings. It tells the reader
where he is standing, gives a verdict fast, and points at one place.

**The seven-principles diagram.** A full-width figure directly under the framing
paragraph, captioned `7 Principles Diagram`. It shows all seven laws in their three
groups, with the focus law highlighted. See `diagram_seven_principles.html`.

**The differentiator paragraph.** One paragraph, after the diagram, on what the company
has that is not the product. In the benchmark it is a team that cares, backed by a
qualified observation about what other founders say.

**A one-line hand-off.** Something like: *Assuming that you already know this, the next
step is to run the growth phase through the seven principles.* Then the sections start.

**Seven principle sections.** `PRINCIPLE 1: Intent & Action` through
`PRINCIPLE 7: Karma & Value`, each a small bold blue heading, each followed by one to
four short paragraphs. Length varies by how much the author actually saw. Principle 1
gets one paragraph. Principle 4 gets five plus a bulleted list plus a full-page diagram.
Principle 6 gets four plus an outside reference. **Do not pad the thin ones to match the
thick ones.** The unevenness is honest and it is part of the voice.

**Opportunity diagrams.** Where one law carries the biggest opportunity, a full-page
figure follows its section. In the benchmark that is `THE MARKETING GARDEN` under
Principle 4: a header card naming the asset that already exists (the website), a short
statement of the law in the client's terms, then six labelled cards, each with a
category word, a name, and one line of what it is for. A footer line ties it back to
the tools.

**Principle 7 as the synthesis.** The last section does not introduce new material. It
opens `The previous 6 principles suggest the following:` and then walks the six earlier
recommendations in order in a single paragraph, ending on a forward-looking statement of
belief about the client's own stated goal.

## 3. How a section is built

Each principle section is assembled from four moves. Not all four appear every time, and
they do not appear in a fixed order.

1. **The observation.** Something the author actually saw or was told, stated plainly and
   attributed to where it came from. *"You mentioned that you are looking to scale up
   outbound communications."* *"Amanda finds the pattern of her work 'therapeutic'."*

2. **The law applied.** The observation read through the law, in the law's own terms but
   without lecturing. *"Building the infrastructure that enables QA efficiency is in
   direct alignment with being considerate about how you deal with your client's
   problems."* The law is never quoted at the reader; it is used.

3. **The opportunity.** What to do, at the size the company actually is. Specific enough
   to start on. *"There may be an opportunity to get ahead of this trend, and recruit
   people that can create a franchise-like brand experience using Clear RCM as the
   back-end."*

4. **The honest gap.** Where the author did not learn enough, he says so and asks. This
   move is what makes the rest credible.

   > Although I did learn about which EHR applications are "winning" and which are
   > "losing" [...] I didn't hear much discussion on that level in terms of client base.
   >
   > - *Who are the success stories? Why?*
   > - *What clients have been problematic? Why?*

## 4. Voice rules the benchmark obeys

Read these as hard constraints. The `7eiu-mo-voice` and `7eiu-human-voice` skills carry
the full rules; these are the ones this document type turns on.

**First person and second person, throughout.** "I", "you". Never "the client",
"the organization", "stakeholders".

**Named people, first names, in the body.** Amanda. Joe. This is an advisory letter,
not a contract, so the contract convention of roles-over-names does not apply. Use the
names the client used. Attach an observation to each name that the client would
recognize as true.

**Claims hedged to what was actually learned.** "it seems", "I suspect", "From my
vantage point", "There may be an opportunity", "may be", "I believe". The confidence
lives in the specifics, not in the verbs.

**Nothing invented, ever.** No statistics, no anecdotes, no clients, no staff, no
"in my experience", no track record. When the author cites something outside the
engagement, it is a real, findable source with a link, and it is used to make one narrow
point. If there is no evidence for a claim, the claim gets cut or turned into a question.

**No AI tool names or version numbers.** Anywhere. The document says "AI tools", "AI
would be well-suited", "these types of solutions". This is the same rule as the book,
and it is what keeps the deliverable from expiring.

**Never name the hidden scaffold.** The seven laws are presented as Mo's framework. The
older tradition behind them is never mentioned.

**Short paragraphs.** Three to six sentences. One-sentence paragraphs are fine.

**Italic bullets for questions and options.** Bulleted lists are italicized and short.
They carry questions back to the client, or a menu of assets. They never carry the
argument.

**Endings are forward-looking, not quotable.** Sections end on their content, on a
question, or on what happens next. No manufactured closing line.

**Client vocabulary, verbatim.** If they say "growth thesis", the document says growth
thesis. If they say "outbound", it says outbound.

## 5. The tiered deliverables

The team produces two documents from one intake.

**The Quick Read (2 pages).** Free, ungated, meant to be given away from the marketing
garden at book.mwright.com. Cover block, a three-sentence framing paragraph, the
seven-principles diagram, and seven blocks of two to three sentences each: one
observation, one opportunity, per law. It closes with the single highest-leverage law
named, and what a full assessment would go after. It draws on the intake record, the same as the full document.

**The Full Assessment (8 to 12 pages).** The benchmark. Everything in section 2 above,
plus an opportunity diagram for the one or two laws carrying the most leverage, plus
the Principle 7 synthesis.

Both are built from the same intake and the same chapter-skill output. The Quick Read is
not a summary written after the full one. It is the same findings at a different
resolution, and it should be produced first, because writing it forces the ranking that
the full document needs.

---

## 6. Hard fails

A draft carrying any of these does not ship, regardless of its score.

- An AI product name or version number.
- An invented person, client, number, statistic, or anecdote.
- A claim about the reader's operations that the intake does not support.
- Hermeticism, or any of the original principle names, mentioned.
- A law applied to the wrong domain (marketing advice filed under Principle 3, and so on).
- A section ending on a manufactured quotable takeaway.
- Any of the seven sections missing.
