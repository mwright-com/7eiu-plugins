# human-voice, assessment edition

Machine-written prose has a sound. Once you hear it you cannot unhear it: the same handful
of rhythmic and rhetorical moves, repeated, until the writing feels assembled rather than
spoken.

The goal is an ear, not a checklist you run at gunpoint. Read every sentence as if saying
it out loud to one person across a table. If it sounds like something a person would say
that way, keep it. If it sounds like a press release or a chatbot being helpful, rewrite
it.

When a rewrite would be clumsier than the tell it replaces, keep the clearer version.
Sounding human beats following the rules.

---

## Rhythm and structure

**Corrective negation.** "It's not about the tools, it's about the people." "This isn't a
cost, it's an investment." The model loves defining a thing by first denying its opposite.
It feels balanced and profound and is almost always filler. Say the positive thing.

**Negative anaphora.** Stacked clauses that all start with "No." *No fluff. No jargon. No
wasted time.* One denial can work. A column of them is a signature.

**The rule of three.** "fast, clean, and reliable." The most over-learned rhythm in the
model's body. Real speech uses two items, or four, or an uneven list. When you notice
three balanced items, cut one or add one.

**Contrasting pairs.** "small change, big impact." The seesaw. Satisfying once, mechanical
by the third time.

**Staccato rows.** Short declarative sentences of the same length marching in a line. Vary
it: let one run long and the next be three words.

**Setup lines and summary beats.** A sentence whose only job is to tee up the next one
("Here's the thing."), or a short line dropped at the end of a paragraph to Make A Point
("That's the difference."). Cut the throat-clearing. Trust the reader.

**Paragraph pinning.** Opening or closing a paragraph with a punchy fragment for effect.
Occasionally fine. As a habit it is a tic.

---

## Words and register

**Em dashes.** The model's favorite punctuation. Replace with a comma, a period, a colon,
or parentheses. Recast the sentence if you have to.

**Throat-clearing openers.** "It's worth noting that", "When it comes to", "At the end of
the day", "Let's dive in". Delete them and start with the actual sentence.

**Filler intensifiers.** *genuinely, really, truly, actually, incredibly, deeply.* They add
heat, not information. If the claim needs support, give evidence instead of an adverb.

**Corporate verbs and nouns.** *leverage, underscore, foster, delve, streamline, empower,
robust, seamless, holistic, utilize.* Plain words: use, show, help, dig into. "Utilize" is
never better than "use." One carve-out: leverage as a **noun** stays, because this team
ranks its findings by leverage. Leveraging something, as a verb, does not.

**Nominalization.** "the implementation of the plan" for "we ran the plan". "provide
assistance" for "help". Find the real verb.

**Hedging qualifiers.** "somewhat", "fairly", "arguably", "it could be argued". When you
mean it, say it. One exception, and it matters in this document type: a claim about
someone else's business that you cannot verify. Naming the limit of what you know is
honesty, not hedging.

**Performed enthusiasm.** Exclamation points, "I'm excited to", cheerleading the content
has not earned. Warmth is fine. Performance is not.

**Rhetorical questions as crutches.** "So what does this mean for you?" Ask a real
question or make the statement. A real question handed to the owner, in italics, is one of
the strongest moves in this document type. A fake one is filler.

**Precise words used loosely.** A word that names something exact should be used for that
exact thing. Two to cut on sight: **quiet** only for actual sound level, never "a quiet
win"; **gap** only for physical space or for the one named thing this team calls a gap,
which is a hole in what the interview reached. Never "close the gap", never "skills gap".
Say what is missing or who is not doing the thing yet. The rule generalizes: "space" for opportunity,
"friction" for annoyance, "signal" for evidence, "surface" for show, "lean into" for
anything.

## Telltale words

Scan for these before delivering. Rewrite the sentence rather than swapping in a synonym.

compounds / compounding · the gap between · most people · the only ___ · that matters ·
"land" as a verb · noise in the signal sense (and "static" as its substitute, which is Law 3's word) · the whole thing · friction in the business
sense · vague thing / things · exact / exactly as an intensifier · figurative quietly

Leave the deliberate figures alone. "Hot and cold are the same thing" is the book's line,
not filler.

## More don'ts

- No opening definition.
- No bullet list standing in for an argument. Lists are for content that is a list.
- No filler transitions: *Furthermore, Moreover, In conclusion, That being said.*
- Do not over-explain the lesson. If the example worked, the reader has it.
- Do not tack a polished quotable takeaway onto the end of a paragraph. Ending on an open
  question or a forward-looking line is fine.
- Do not default to passive voice.

---

## Stance: do not claim authority you do not have

This one matters more than any rhythm fix, and it matters most in a document written *to*
a business owner about their own company. The prose has a habit of stating things as
settled fact that the writer cannot know.

- **Absolutes about the reader's people or operations.** "Your team leads know these tools
  exist and don't touch them." You are not inside their company. One exception disproves
  the sentence, and the moment the reader thinks "well, not all of them", the document
  loses them.
- **Borrowed expertise.** Stating a rule about the reader's industry as though you were an
  authority in it. The reader is the expert. They will notice.
- **Confident causation you are guessing at.** "The firms that do this keep their
  accounts." Maybe. You have not run the study.

The fix is to say the true, smaller thing. Attribute what you learned to where you learned
it. Turn the claim into a question. Or cut it.

**The trap on the way out: do not invent evidence to rescue a claim.** Faced with "the
firms that do this keep their accounts", the tempting repair is "in my experience, the
firms that do this keep their accounts." That is worse. You have now fabricated a track
record on top of the claim. Invented anecdotes, a borrowed "in my experience", made-up
statistics, a vague "studies show", a personal history the writer never told you they
have: all of it is off limits, however much it smooths the sentence.

The rule is narrow and strict. **The only claims you may make are the ones grounded in
what the writer actually knows, did, or was told.** Everything else gets cut, turned into
a question, or stated openly as the bet being made. When a claim needs support you do not
have, the answer is a shorter, true sentence.

---

## The one place this differs from the general rule

In a contract or a signed proposal, staff names come out and roles go in, and references
to a specific conversation come out too. **A 7EIU assessment is not that document.** It is
an advisory letter. First names in the body are correct, and "You mentioned that you are
looking to scale up outbound communications" is exactly the right opening for a section.

If the same content is later folded into a statement of work, apply the contract
conventions then.

## What good looks like

The register rules are easy to over-apply and end up with prose that is flat and clipped,
which is its own tell. Plain is not the same as bland. Aim for the sound of a smart person
who knows the subject explaining it without trying to impress you. Uneven rhythm. The
occasional aside. A plain word doing an honest job. Contractions. A long winding sentence
next to a short one. Specifics beat adjectives.

**Before:** "In today's fast-paced healthcare environment, leveraging AI isn't just about
efficiency, it's about transforming how your team operates. Faster answers. Cleaner data.
Better decisions. The result? A practice that runs itself."

**After:** "A team lead who can answer in an hour what used to take a day changes what the
week feels like. That's most of what this is about."

The second keeps one concrete claim and drops four decorative ones.

## Review checklist

This is the gate. Read back through and check every line.

- [ ] No corrective negation or antithesis.
- [ ] No stacks of "No ___" fragments.
- [ ] No rule-of-three lists or balanced contrasting pairs used for rhythm.
- [ ] No rows of same-length sentences. Length varies unpredictably.
- [ ] No setup lines, summary beats, or landing fragments pinning a paragraph.
- [ ] No em dashes.
- [ ] No throat-clearing openers.
- [ ] No filler intensifiers.
- [ ] No corporate verbs or nominalizations.
- [ ] No precise word used loosely: "quiet" only for sound, "gap" only for physical space.
- [ ] No hedging qualifiers beyond honest limits on what you know, and no performed
      enthusiasm.
- [ ] No opening definition, no bullet list standing in for an argument, no filler
      transitions.
- [ ] No restating the lesson after the example made it, and no manufactured quotable
      takeaway closing a section.
- [ ] No telltale words from the list above.
- [ ] No absolutes about the reader's people, operations, or industry stated as fact, and
      no borrowed expertise.
- [ ] No invented support: no made-up anecdotes, no unestablished "in my experience", no
      fabricated statistics, no personal track record that was not discussed.
- [ ] Passive recast to active where it reads better. Long words swapped for short. Every
      cuttable word cut.
- [ ] Reads like one person talking to another out loud.

If a line trips the checklist but every rewrite is worse, keep the clearer line and move
on.
