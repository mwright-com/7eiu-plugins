## The law

What works for big companies can work for small ones, and what works for small ones can work for big ones.

The book's image is a snowflake and a glacier. Different size, different speed, very different consequences if one falls on you. Same chemistry all the way across, same crystal, same rules of water and cold. Understand how one snowflake forms and you understand something true about the glacier, and it runs the other direction too.

This law governs process. The repeatable patterns of the work. What can be scaled up, what has to be protected as it scales, and what a company can take from a scale above or below it instead of inventing from nothing.

## What this law is looking for in a business

Signals the law is being obeyed:

- The owner can name a specific thing a much larger company does and describe how a smaller version of it would run here.
- The owner can name a specific thing this company does that the giants in the line of work cannot do anymore.
- Parts of the work that repeat have been separated from the parts that need a person each time, and somebody can say which is which.
- A method one person carries in their head has been written down at least once so a second person could run it.
- Somebody on the team has already taken a pattern from outside and shrunk it to fit, without being told to.
- The owner talks about growth in terms of keeping something, and can name what that something is.
- New volume goes to a written pattern before it goes to a heroic effort.
- The team uses tools to do the department-sized version of a job the owner used to skip.

Signals the law is being broken:

- The owner believes they have to invent everything, and treats every problem as new.
- The owner copies a big company's surface appearance, the look and the language, instead of the process underneath it.
- The owner dismisses everything big companies do as irrelevant at this size.
- Work bottlenecks at one person as volume rises, and everybody already knows which person.
- Small-shop instincts, knowing customers by name, changing the offer in an afternoon, are treated as things to be outgrown.
- Nothing is written down, so nothing can be handed off, supported by a tool, or scaled.
- Two people do the same job in two different ways and neither knows the other's way.
- Growth plans are stated as headcount only, with no pattern attached to the new heads.
- The owner studies competitors of the same size and nobody above or below.

## What to ask

From Round 3 of the intake interview:

- What do the biggest companies in your line of work do that you cannot?
- What do you do that they cannot, or no longer do?
- Which parts of the work are the same every time, and which need a person each time?
- Who on the team already uses AI tools, and for what? Who does not, and why?
- Where does the work bottleneck when volume goes up?

Probes to add, and ask the translation question in both directions, because a growing company needs both:

- Pick one large company you admire. What is the one thing it does well that you would steal if you could? Then: what would the one-person version of that look like here?
- If you doubled your volume next quarter, what is the first thing that breaks, and whose desk is it on?
- Name the person here who is best at the part of the work that repeats. What do they do that nobody has ever written down?
- What would you be sad to lose about how this place works today, if you were twice the size?

Mark every answer `[said]`, `[doc]`, `[observed]` or `[gap]`. A pattern you infer from a website is not a `[said]`, and it cannot become a claim.

## Opportunity patterns

**1. The translation habit on a schedule.**
Missing when the owner has no routine for looking at another scale, and improvement arrives only when something goes wrong.

- One-person shop: once a month, pick one large company in the line of work and one specific thing it does well. Follow-up after a purchase. How it decides what to make next. How it handles a complaint. Write the one-person version of it on a page and run it for thirty days.
- Team of ten: give the monthly translation to a rotating person, one hour, and have them bring back one pattern the team could run at this size. Keep the page.
- Scaling or acquiring: run it in both directions. Study a large operator for pattern, and study a one-person shop in the same field for the instinct you have started to lose.

**2. Tacit method to written pattern.**
Missing when the answer to "how do we do that" is a person's name.

Law 2 owns writing the method down when the point is that it can be handed off, run at another size, or survive a sale. Law 3 owns writing the standard down when the point is that the output is the same every time. Keep this one on portability, and hand the sameness question to Law 3 in a clause without arguing it here.

- One-person shop: pick the job you do most often and narrate it into a recording while you do it. Turn that into a numbered page this week, written so a person who is not you could pick it up and run it.
- Team of ten: for each person, write down the one method they own, in the form a second person would need to take it over. An afternoon each. A tool can turn the recording into a first draft that the person then corrects, which is faster than asking them to write from a blank page.
- Scaling or acquiring: the written pattern is what you are actually buying and selling. Make it a condition of the deal that the method exists on paper before the person's last day, and check that the page still holds at the volume the next size brings.

**3. The customer-research department, shrunk.**
Missing when the owner has strong opinions about customers and no place those opinions came from.

- One-person shop: one focused hour a week. Take the mail, reviews, and complaints already sitting in the inbox and have a tool sort them into recurring themes. Read the themes yourself. Change one thing.
- Team of ten: same hour, but the output goes to the whole team in a short note, and one theme becomes an action with a name on it.
- Scaling or acquiring: run it across every acquired location so you hear the new customers before the integration decisions are made.

**4. Protecting a small-shop instinct on purpose.**
Missing when the owner cannot name what they want to keep, or names it and has no mechanism holding it.

- One-person shop: write down the instinct while you still have it. What you do that a larger operator would not.
- Team of ten: name one instinct, say who owns it, and put a check on it. If the instinct is that customers hear back the same day, someone watches the same-day number.
- Scaling or acquiring: write the instinct into onboarding for new sites, and let a tool carry the reach so the instinct does not have to be rationed as volume rises.

**5. The one person who caps the company.**
Missing when volume rises and everybody waits on the same desk.

- One-person shop: that person is you. Take the single most repeated task off your desk this month.
- Team of ten: name the role, then give that role support first, before hiring anywhere else. Support usually means the method written down plus a tool doing the first pass.
- Scaling or acquiring: the cap moves as you grow. Look again every quarter, because the person who capped you at ten is rarely the person who caps you at forty.

**6. Sizing the pattern honestly.**
Missing when the owner adopted a large company's process whole and it now costs more than the work it governs.

- One-person shop: strip it back to the one step that carried the result.
- Team of ten: keep the step that produces the decision, drop the reporting layer built to inform people you do not have.
- Scaling or acquiring: add the layer back one piece at a time as there are actually people who need it.

Every recommendation you write has to be startable inside a month. If it needs a hire, a budget cycle, or a system nobody has bought yet, shrink it until it starts on a Tuesday.

## The AI angle

The patterns the giants run were never secret. They are visible, studied, and written about at length. The barrier was the resources it took to run them. A data team. A media budget. A department of people whose only job was that one thing. That barrier has come down, so a pattern the owner could always see is now a pattern the owner can afford to run.

Say what the tools do, in the two directions this law cares about. They are good at giving a large slow body small fast instincts back, letting an operation that stopped hearing its customers hear all of them again. They are also good at giving a small operation the reach that used to need a department, so one person can run the follow-up, the sorting, and the research that used to take a floor of people.

The translation is still done by a person. Somebody has to understand what made the pattern work before it can be shrunk, and a tool that copies the surface of a large company's process will hand a small company an expensive imitation. So when you find a pattern worth bringing down, ask the owner what they think makes it work at the size it came from, and build the recommendation out of that answer rather than out of the pattern's surface.

Never name an AI product, company, or version number. Write "AI tools", "these types of solutions", "a tool that can". A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 2 section

Heading: `PRINCIPLE 2: Big & Small`

Length: one to four short paragraphs. If you only saw one thing, write one paragraph. Do not pad it to match a longer section.

Use the four moves from the benchmark spec, in whatever order the material wants, and not all four every time: the observation, the law applied, the opportunity, the honest gap.

Open on something the owner actually said, and where you can, open on a specific person and what that person is good at. This law comes down on individuals doing repeatable work, so a name with an observation attached is the strongest first line you have. If the intake gave you no names, open on the piece of the work that repeats.

Vocabulary this law owns: snowflake and glacier, scale, translation, pattern, the version of a thing at another size. You do not have to use the snowflake and the glacier by name in every section, and it reads better used once with weight than sprinkled.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| Law | Its images |
|---|---|
| 1. Intention and Action | the mirror, the drill and the hole, intention before action, the conversation |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 4. Active and Passive Marketing | active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil |
| 5. Wins and Losses | hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the continuous scale |
| 6. Rhythm and Fit | rhythm, the swing, the kite, the wind, the tide, the arc, fit |
| 7. Karma and Value | the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf, giving and extracting, the bay, the net |

That is the whole table minus Law 2's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: this law's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

You may name another law when a finding hands off to it. You may not argue that finding again in your own imagery.

First person and second person. "I", "you". Hedge to what you actually learned: "it seems", "I suspect", "there may be an opportunity". Short paragraphs. End on the content or on a question.

## Worked example

The benchmark's PRINCIPLE 2 section read in full:

> Building the infrastructure that enables QA efficiency is in direct alignment with being considerate about how you deal with your client's problems.
>
> Amanda finds the pattern of her work "therapeutic". Giving her access to AI tools, as well as training with them, I suspect, will greatly increase her performance in the area of QA. This will be a strong lever for handling increased growth.
>
> Joe is adept at AI. She is willing to learn and is proactive at doing so. Her needs are simply to have a guide that will keep her from feeling like she's on an island. When she runs into obstacles, having someone she can reliably go to in order to give her a larger perspective of what the AI tools are capable of will enhance her ability to build useful workflows. Further enabling growth by providing higher bandwidth processes for the office.
>
> These are traits that will work effectively at the company's current size, as well as when the company grows.

What it is doing:

- It names two real people by their first names, Amanda and Joe, both of whom came out of the intake.
- Each name carries one observation the person would recognize as true about herself. Amanda finds the pattern therapeutic. Joe is proactive and teaches herself.
- "Therapeutic" is Amanda's word, in quotation marks, kept verbatim.
- It hedges. "I suspect" sits in the middle of the strongest claim in the section, which is what makes the claim usable.
- Joe's paragraph names a specific need, a guide she can go to, rather than a training program or a platform.
- Every observation is tied to the scaling question. Amanda's QA work is a lever for handling growth. Joe's workflows give the office higher bandwidth.
- The closing sentence is the law stated plainly, that these traits work at this size and at the next one, and the law is never quoted at the reader.
- It never names a tool. It says "AI tools" twice and stops there.

Now an illustration. **The company below is fictional, invented for this file, and must never be presented to a client as a real engagement.**

Valley Ridge Roasters, a six-person specialty coffee roaster. Verdict: `working`.

> You told me Devin can taste a batch and tell you which drum the beans came off of, and that he does the roast log by memory because writing it down slows him down. That is the part of your work that repeats, sitting entirely inside one person. When you talked about adding the second roaster next spring, Devin's name came up four times.
>
> There may be an opportunity in both directions here. The wholesale roasters you mentioned admiring run a post-delivery follow-up on every account, a short check on how the coffee performed that week, and it is the reason they hear about a problem before the account goes quiet. The version of that you could run this month is one hour, one message to every wholesale account, and a tool sorting the replies into what keeps coming up. Going the other way, what Devin does by taste is a pattern, and patterns can be written. I would sit with him for an afternoon, record him talking through three roasts, and let a tool turn that into a first draft of a roast log he can correct. That page is what makes a second roaster possible without turning your coffee into someone else's.

## What you hand back

Return the five-part block from the chapter-skill contract. Add `diagram` only if this law carries the biggest opportunity in the engagement.

Add `blocked_on` when the recommendation here is right but cannot be started until something else changes. It names the law whose finding has to move first. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

`working`

**section**
The two paragraphs above, ready to drop under `PRINCIPLE 2: Big & Small`.

**quick_read**
Devin holds your roast method by memory, which is also where your growth stops. Writing that method down, and running a shrunk version of the wholesale follow-up the larger roasters use, are both startable this month.

**opportunities**
1. Record Devin talking through three roasts and turn it into a correctable written roast log.
2. One hour a week on wholesale follow-up, replies sorted into recurring themes by a tool.
3. Name the instinct you want to keep at twelve people, and put one number on it.

**gaps**
I did not get a clear picture of how the two retail baristas learn the pour standard, or whether anything about it is written.

- *Who trained the last barista, and what did they hand them?*
- *If Devin were out for three weeks, what happens to the roast schedule?*

## Self-check

Before handing the block back, walk this list.

1. No AI product name, company name, or version number anywhere in the section, the quick read, or the opportunities.
2. Every person named traces to a name in the intake record, with the observation attached to a `[said]` or `[observed]` entry.
3. Any quoted word is the owner's or the team member's own word, marked as theirs.
4. Only Law 2's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
5. Every recommendation names a specific pattern from a specific kind of company. "Study the market leaders" is not a recommendation.
6. Every recommendation can be started within a month, without a hire or a budget cycle.
7. The translation question appears in both directions if the company is growing.
8. The section is as long as the material and no longer. Nothing was added to match another section's length.
9. Nothing in the section is a plausible inference. Every claim traces to the intake, or it was turned into a question in `gaps`.
10. First person and second person throughout. No "the client", no "the organization".
11. The strongest claim carries a hedge, and the specifics carry the confidence.
12. The last sentence ends on content, a next step, or a question, and not on a manufactured takeaway.
