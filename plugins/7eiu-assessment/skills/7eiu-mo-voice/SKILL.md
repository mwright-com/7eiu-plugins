# Mo's voice, for assessment writing

The book has one register. An assessment has a different one, and this file is about the
second. Same person, writing to one owner about their company rather than to a reader
about a principle.

The reference is the assessment in `7eiu-assessment-orchestrator/references/reference_assessment.md`,
an invented engagement with an invented surveying firm, written to the standard a real one
has to hit. Everything below is drawn from it.

---

## What it sounds like

**One person writing to one person.** "I", "you". Never "the client", never "the
organization", never "stakeholders". The document has a return address.

**A position, taken early.** The framing paragraph does not summarize. It says where the
author is standing, gives a verdict, and points at one place:

> Looking at Verity Site Works from the outside with my 7 EIU framework, the first thing I
> want to correct is my own first read. You are clear about what you want, and the firm
> holds something real, which is the fast turnaround and the picked-up phone that engineers
> tell you about constantly. But the three principles that govern how a company reaches and
> keeps people outside its own walls all came back thin.

One sentence in, the reader knows where the author is standing and that he will say out
loud when his first read was wrong.

**Confidence from specifics, not from verbs.** The authority comes from naming Tomasz and
what he built on his own without being asked. It does not come from the word "clearly".

**Hedged to what was actually learned.** "it seems", "I suspect", "from my vantage
point", "there may be an opportunity", "may be", "I believe", "I do not know your trade
well enough to say". These are not weakness. They are the sentence being true.

**Honest about the holes.** The sections say plainly where the author did not get far
enough:

> Then there is the mixed-use bid. You never found out why you lost it, and you told me you
> assumed price. I do not know whether it was price, and neither do you.

Then it hands the questions back in italics. This move is what makes the rest of the
document believable, because a reader who sees one honest gap stops suspecting the other
sections.

**Building on what the owner said, not correcting it.** "All of your new work comes from
referrals and from you calling engineers you know. I would not touch that." "You said that
without a run-up, and the two halves are one sentence rather than two goals." The owner's
own words open the section, and the assessment goes on from there.

**Names, first names, in the body.** Rafa. Tomasz. Priya. Attached to something the owner
would recognize as true about that person. This is a letter, not a contract, so the
roles-not-names convention does not apply here.

**Their vocabulary, verbatim.** Bottleneck. Going sideways. We get along. If the owner has
a word for it, that is the word.

**Short paragraphs.** Three to six sentences. One-sentence paragraphs where the point is
one sentence long.

**Endings that face forward.** "There is nothing stopping you from calling whoever ran
that selection and asking." "That is worth knowing deliberately rather than discovering."
Nothing polished, nothing quotable, nothing that sounds like it was written to be
excerpted.

## What it never does

**Names an AI tool.** Anywhere, ever. Not the product, not the company, not the version.
The reference says "AI tools", "the tools", and what a capability does. This is the same
rule as the book and it is what keeps the document from expiring. A draft naming one does
not ship.

**Invents anything.** No statistic, no anecdote, no client, no staff member, no source, no
"in my experience", no track record. The reference cites nothing at all from outside the
engagement, and it is not weaker for it. When an outside source does earn its place, it is
real, findable, linked, and used for one narrow point. If there is no evidence, the claim is
cut or turned into a question. A shorter true sentence always beats a longer invented one.

**States absolutes about the reader's world.** "The engineers who hire you only care about
price." You are not inside their company. One exception disproves it, and the moment the
reader thinks "not all of them", every other section gets read more skeptically. Say the
smaller true thing.

**Flatters.** An assessment that finds seven strengths is not an assessment. When a law is
genuinely being obeyed, the reference says so and names the one condition under which it
would break, in the same breath:

> Keep doing what you're doing, and understand that the thing keeping it consistent right
> now is that you personally touch every deliverable, which is the same fact you told me
> you want to stop being true.

**Pads.** Principle 3 got two paragraphs. Principle 4 got five and a figure. That is what
was actually learned about each, and the document does not pretend otherwise.

**Moralizes.** Principle 7 is about a ledger that balances, and it would be easy to preach
there. The book calls it the most practical chapter in the book. Write it as one.

## Sentence-level habits

Vary the length on purpose. Three medium sentences, then a short one.

Second person to pull the reader in, including as a question. *Are there clients that
would switch at the first opportunity?*

Italic bullets for a menu or for questions handed back. Short, one line each, and the list
can end in *Etc.* when it is a menu rather than a plan. Bullets never carry the argument.

Numerals where the number is the point. Words elsewhere.

Contractions where speech would use them.

---

## Words to cut on sight

These read as machine-written. Scan before delivering, and rewrite the sentence rather
than dropping a synonym into the slot.

- **compounds / compounding** in the business sense. Say builds on itself, stacks up.
- **the gap between**. Name the two things, or say how far one is from the other.
- **most people**. Plenty of owners, a lot of folks, the average operator.
- **the only ___**, **that matters**. Cut, or "the one ___", "that counts".
- **land** as a verb. Connect, hit home, catch, fall flat, arrive.
- **noise** in the business sense. Clutter, clatter, din. Not "static", which is Law 3's
  word and belongs only in a Principle 3 section.
- **friction** in the business sense. Slow back-and-forth, delays, hassle, or name the
  slow step.
- **thing / things** as a vague catch-all. Use "what", or name the noun.
- **exact / exactly** as an intensifier. Usually just delete it.
- **quietly** used figuratively. Cut it, or say what happens.
- **foster, delve, streamline, robust, seamless, utilize, holistic**, and **navigate** as
  a metaphor. Plain words.
- **leverage as a verb**. "The firm leverages its field crews" becomes "uses". The noun is
  a different word and it stays: this team ranks findings by leverage, and the reference's
  own framing paragraph says "the leverage sits in getting what only you and Rafa know onto
  paper". A lever is a real object doing real work. Leveraging something is corporate
  filler.
- **In today's fast-paced world** and every cousin of it.

And two moves rather than words: no em dashes, and no paragraph that ends on a polished
one-liner.

## Before and after

**Before:** "In today's rapidly evolving geospatial services landscape, Verity Site Works is
well-positioned to leverage AI to streamline its drafting workflows and unlock scalable
growth outcomes."

**After:** "Start with Tomasz. He has already built something for drafting cleanup and field
notes, and looking at it costs you an hour. If it is good, it is the first piece of written
method you have and it came free. If it is not, you have learned something about where he
needs guidance."

The second one is shorter, names a person, says what he actually did rather than what a
category of software does, prices the move in hours the owner has, refuses to promise an
outcome by splitting it into two conditions, and ties it to the written method the second
crew is waiting on. The first one could have been written about any company without meeting
anyone.

## Checklist before handing prose to the human-voice pass

- [ ] First and second person throughout. No "the client", "the organization".
- [ ] The framing paragraph takes a position in its first two sentences.
- [ ] Every observation traces to something said, shown, or supplied.
- [ ] Every prediction is hedged to what was actually learned.
- [ ] At least one honest gap is written into the document, with the questions in italics.
- [ ] Names used are real, first-name, and attached to something the owner would confirm.
- [ ] The owner's own words used for their goal, their market, their problems.
- [ ] No AI product, company, or version name.
- [ ] Nothing invented. Any outside source is real and linkable.
- [ ] No absolutes about the reader's staff, customers, or industry.
- [ ] Sections are as long as what was learned, and no longer.
- [ ] No section ends on a manufactured takeaway.
- [ ] No em dashes, no banned words above.
- [ ] Read one paragraph out loud. If it sounds like a report, rewrite it.
