## The law

What you put into the world comes back to you, shaped by what you put in.

There is no such thing as a free loaf of bread. Somebody grew the wheat, somebody ground it, somebody woke up at four in the morning so it would be warm by seven. When you get something for nothing it only means the bill went to someone else, or it is still coming to you, with interest. The universe keeps a ledger, and it is slow, patient and perfectly accurate. In the book's parable, Cole and Wren buy the same net from the same trader. Cole uses it to drag the bay empty. Wren uses it to take only what she needs and to tend the reef, and five seasons later her half-empty boat comes home full.

This law governs evaluation. Whether the company creates value and captures part of it, or extracts value and disguises it, and what the record of that will look like in five years, when the reviews and the screenshots and the searchable memory of the internet are still sitting there, findable.

## The two jobs of this section

This section does two things at once, and it is the only one of the seven that does. Say this to yourself before you write a word, because the two jobs want different first sentences.

**Job one, the assessment.** Read the company on the ledger, the same way the other six skills read theirs. Is the customer genuinely better off, or do they feel better off until they look closely? Is there money coming in that the customer would object to if they could see how it was earned? Is there a shortcut currently under consideration?

**Job two, the synthesis.** Close the whole document. In the reference assessment, Principle 7 introduced no new material at all. It opened `The previous 6 principles suggest the following:` and walked the six earlier recommendations in order, in a single paragraph, in the owner's own terms, ending on a forward-looking statement of belief about the goal the owner had named in the interview.

**Which one leads.** The test is one question: did the intake surface anything on the extraction side?

If it did not, the synthesis leads. Say the company is clean on the ledger in one clause inside the opening line, then go straight to the six. This is the common case, because a business worth assessing is usually a business that is already giving more than it takes. The reference assessment did this with one variation worth copying. The owner had settled the ledger question himself, in a sentence flat enough to quote back to him, so it took one short paragraph ahead of the hand-off line and the rest of the section went to the walk-through.

If it did, the value finding leads. Write it first, plainly, in its own paragraph, name the specific thing the owner told you, and then run the synthesis after it. Do not bury a shortcut inside the walk-through. Do not soften it into a general observation about the market either. The owner told you about it, which means they already know, and the useful thing you can do is say it once in the open and then show them the six moves that make it unnecessary.

Anything short of an actual extraction finding is not a reason to lead with job one. A market full of new entrants doing cheap things is a Law 6 observation. A customer who left angry is Law 5. Those hand off; they do not lead here.

## What this law is looking for in a business

Signals the law is being obeyed:

- The owner can say where the money comes from and what the customer gets for it, in one breath, without qualifying it.
- The owner would be comfortable if a customer watched everything the company did and why.
- Growth so far came from customers coming back and bringing people, and the owner can point at which ones.
- Somebody on the team has turned down work, or refused a tactic, and the owner tells that story with some pride.
- The company gives something away that costs it real time and returns nothing directly.
- What the owner wants still true in ten years is about the customer or the team, not about the size of the company.
- Staff stay, and the people who left say reasonable things about the place.
- The owner treats a customer's bad outcome as their own problem even when the contract says otherwise.

Signals the law is being broken:

- Growth that was bought rather than earned. The numbers moved and nobody can name the customers behind the movement.
- Generated volume standing in for substance. Output shipped by the hundred because it is cheap, not because anyone asked for it.
- The owner is counting on customers not noticing the difference between the appearance and the substance.
- A shortcut is currently under consideration that the owner would not want a customer to see.
- The numbers look better this month at the customer's expense, and someone has already noticed and said nothing.
- Revenue depends on a steady supply of new people who have not heard yet, because the old ones do not return.
- The pitch and the delivery are two different products, and the team knows which one they are actually shipping.
- What people in the market would say about the company outside the room is not what the owner would say inside it.

## What to ask

From Round 8 of the intake interview:

- Would you be comfortable if a customer could see everything you do and why?
- Where does the money actually come from, and what does the customer get for it?
- Where are you tempted to take a shortcut right now?
- What would people say about you in this market if you were not in the room?
- What do you want to still be true about this business in ten years?

Probes to add:

- Take the last thing you shipped. Is the person who received it better off, or do they only feel better off until they look closely?
- What is the cheapest thing you could do this quarter to make the numbers move, that you have decided not to do? Why not?
- Who inside the company would tell you if something crossed the line, and has anyone ever done it?
- Beyond the customer, who else is on your ledger? Name your people and your community, and say what they get from you being here.

The book's two filter questions carry this round, so put them in front and let the rest follow the answers. The first asks whether the person receiving the work is better off, or only feels better off until they look closely. The second asks whether the owner would be comfortable if the person on the other end could see what they are doing and why. The second question is about what the company is actually doing, not about what it is showing. That distinction is the whole question, so if the owner answers about their messaging, ask again about their operations.

Mark every answer `[said]`, `[doc]`, `[observed]` or `[gap]`. An accusation is the most expensive thing you can put in this document, so an extraction finding needs a `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` behind it and nothing weaker. A suspicion goes in `gaps` as a question, or it goes nowhere.

## Opportunity patterns

**1. The filter question as a standing habit.**
Missing when the owner has good instincts and no place those instincts get applied on a schedule, so the question only gets asked after something has already gone wrong.

- One-person shop: put one question at the top of the page where you plan your week. Does this make my customer's life better, or does it just make my numbers better this month at my customer's expense? Run every decision on the list through it before you start.
- Team of ten: the question goes on the agenda of whatever meeting already exists. One item, five minutes, one decision from that week held up to it out loud. The point is that the team hears the owner ask it.
- Scaling or acquiring: ask it about the acquired operation before the integration decisions get made, and write the answer down while you still have outside eyes.

**2. Taking the position the flood is about to make available.**
Missing when the owner is competing on the same ground as everyone else in a market that is filling up with cheap output.

- One-person shop: pick the one thing you do that a generated version cannot do, and put it where a stranger can see it. Say plainly how the work is made and who makes it.
- Team of ten: make the standard explicit and publish it. What you will not send, what a customer can always expect, who signs off. Then hold to it when a week gets tight.
- Scaling or acquiring: this becomes the brand argument in a market where everyone else's volume looks the same. Get the evidence collected now, from real customers, while the contrast is still worth pointing at.

**3. Being a good corporate citizen the workforce and community will champion.**
Missing when the ledger is being run for customers only, and the people doing the work are treated as a cost of serving them.

- One-person shop: pick one thing you give the community that costs you real time. One a quarter is enough, and doing it consistently beats doing it big.
- Team of ten: ask your people what they would want this place to be known for, then fund one of the answers. Tools that take repetitive work off a person's desk belong here too, because giving someone their afternoon back is an entry on the same ledger.
- Scaling or acquiring: write what you will not change about how people are treated into the integration plan, before the first close, and tell the acquired team what it says.

**4. Refusing the cheap ways to fake traction.**
Missing when the tools have put manufactured reviews, generated volume and pretend-personal outreach within easy reach, and nobody has said out loud that this company does not do that.

- One-person shop: decide once, now, before the first slow month, and write the decision down where you will see it in the slow month.
- Team of ten: name the specific tactics you are declining, so that a junior person under pressure knows which door is closed. A rule nobody has said is not a rule.
- Scaling or acquiring: check what the operation you are buying has been doing. Bought growth is a liability you inherit, and the ledger follows the business, not the previous owner.

**5. Making the record legible.**
Missing when the company has been giving more than it takes for years and none of it is written anywhere a stranger can find.

- One-person shop: ask three customers what changed for them because of your work, and keep their words.
- Team of ten: put a small habit on someone's calendar for collecting outcomes as they happen. This hands off to Law 5, which owns the wins and losses work; here it matters because a good record that nobody can see does not do the company any good.
- Scaling or acquiring: the reserve of trust is the asset a competitor with a better tool cannot copy. Document it the way you would document any other asset in a deal.

Every recommendation you write has to be startable inside a month. If it needs a budget cycle or a hire, shrink it until it starts on a Tuesday.

## The AI angle

For most of history the two paths cost about the same to attempt. Both were hard and slow. The tools broke that symmetry by making the extraction path cheap and fast. An ocean of plausible, empty output now costs almost nothing to produce, and plenty of operators are going to take that bait. Write it that way when it belongs in a section, and write it about the market, not about the reader, unless the intake gave you something about the reader.

The machine cannot tell value from the appearance of value. The value lives in the effect the work has on the person receiving it, and that effect is not visible from inside the output. So the owner holds the standard the machine cannot. This is the practical point of the section and it is worth one plain sentence when you have somewhere to attach it.

The same tools work just as well the other way. An owner who chooses to give can now produce real value at a scale that used to need a hundred people. And because so much of what is coming will be substandard, genuine work is about to be obvious by contrast. That is the opportunity in this law, and for a company that is clean on the ledger it is usually the only thing worth saying about AI here, because the other six sections have already covered the mechanics.

Never name an AI product, company, or version number. Write "AI tools", "these types of solutions", "the tools". A shipped draft naming one is a hard fail.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 7 section

Heading: `PRINCIPLE 7: Karma & Value`

When the synthesis leads, the structure has three parts and nothing else.

1. **The hand-off line.** One line that turns the document toward its close. The reference assessment used `The previous 6 principles suggest the following:` and there is no reason to improve on it. If the company is clean on the ledger, that clause goes in the same sentence and does not get its own paragraph, unless the owner settled the question with a line worth quoting back to him. Then it gets one short paragraph ahead of the hand-off line and nothing more, the way the reference does.
2. **The walk-through paragraph.** One paragraph, in the owner's own terms, naming the six moves in law order, one after another. Principle 1 first, Principle 6 last. Short clauses. Use the owner's vocabulary verbatim, the way the reference assessment used the owner's own words. Do not label the principles by number inside the paragraph; the reader knows what order the document went in.
3. **The closing belief statement.** One sentence about the owner reaching the goal they named in the interview, in the owner's own words for that goal.

The synthesis paragraph must not introduce anything the earlier sections did not already establish. No new opportunity, no new observation, no new gap. If something belongs in the document and is not in one of the first six sections, it goes back to the skill that owns it, not here. When you find yourself wanting to add a seventh item, that is the signal that a section upstream is incomplete.

The closing belief statement must reference the owner's stated goal verbatim, in the words they used in Round 9. Not a paraphrase, not a tidied-up version. It carries a hedge, because "I believe" is what keeps it a judgment instead of a promise. And it is not a slogan. If the sentence would work for any company, delete it and write one that only works for this one.

When the value finding leads, put it first as its own paragraph, then run the three parts above after it.

Length: two to four short paragraphs. This section does not get long. The document has already said everything it has to say.

Vocabulary this law owns: the ledger, cause and effect, the free loaf of bread, giving and extracting, the bay, the net, the record with a long memory. Use one image with weight rather than several in passing. The ledger usually does all the work by itself.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

| Law | Its images |
|---|---|
| 1. Intention and Action | the mirror, the drill and the hole, intention before action, the conversation |
| 2. Big and Small | the snowflake and the glacier, translation, the pattern, figurative scale |
| 3. Being Consistent | frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, static, holding steady |
| 4. Active and Passive Marketing | active and passive, the fire and the garden, hunter and gardener, seed and soil |
| 5. Wins and Losses | hot and cold, temperature, the coin, the autopsy, the continuous scale |
| 6. Rhythm and Fit | rhythm, the swing, the kite, the wind, the tide, the arc, fit |

That is the whole table minus Law 7's own row. Do not carry a shortened version of it into your head; a short list is how an image goes missing and then turns up in a section.

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is Law 3's alone.

Name another law when a finding hands off to it. Do not argue that finding again in your own imagery.

First person and second person throughout. Short paragraphs. This is the most practical chapter in the book, so the section reads as practical. It does not preach.

## Worked example

The reference assessment's PRINCIPLE 7 section, in full. Verity Site Works is invented, and so is everyone in it.

> When I asked where you would be tempted to take a shortcut, you said "No. I stamp it, it's my license. That's the whole business." That settles the ledger question for a firm like this one, so I will spend the rest of this section on the other six.
>
> The previous 6 principles suggest the following:
>
> Say the outcome you gave me out loud to Rafa, Tomasz and Priya, and find out whether it comes back the same way. Get what is in your head and Rafa's onto paper, starting with how a job gets set up, run and checked, and look at what Tomasz has already built before you build anything next to it. Hold the fast turnaround and the picked-up phone, and understand that right now it is held by you personally touching everything. Put one thing in the open besides the contact form, so the two weeks of ringing phone you described is not the whole of what happens without you. Find out why Cordero + Ash keeps coming back, and make the call on the mixed-use job you lost. Then start watching the two firms advertising drone and lidar, and decide what it means rather than finding out from a bid sheet. All of it is startable this month, and the tools carry a real share of the labor on every piece once you have decided what you are actually making. I believe that gets you to the point where you are "not the bottleneck on every deliverable," and to a second crew "without the quality going sideways," which is how you put it to me.

The opening paragraph settles the ledger and stops. The owner's refusal is quoted rather than summarized, the assessor agrees with it in half a sentence, and then says out loud what the rest of the section is going to be. No praise is attached to it and no lesson is drawn from it.

Map each clause of the walk-through back to where it came from:

- "Say the outcome you gave me out loud to Rafa, Tomasz and Priya, and find out whether it comes back the same way" is Principle 1. The intention exists and it has never left the owner's head.
- "Get what is in your head and Rafa's onto paper, starting with how a job gets set up, run and checked, and look at what Tomasz has already built before you build anything next to it" is Principle 2, and it is the longest clause because it was the heaviest finding in the document.
- "Hold the fast turnaround and the picked-up phone, and understand that right now it is held by you personally touching everything" is Principle 3, strength and condition in one clause.
- "Put one thing in the open besides the contact form, so the two weeks of ringing phone you described is not the whole of what happens without you" is Principle 4.
- "Find out why Cordero + Ash keeps coming back, and make the call on the mixed-use job you lost" is Principle 5, two moves compressed into fourteen words.
- "Then start watching the two firms advertising drone and lidar, and decide what it means rather than finding out from a bid sheet" is Principle 6.

Then note what else it does. The six are in law order and the reader can feel the document's own sequence in the sentence order. "All of it is startable this month" is the practicality claim, and it earns its place because every one of the six was written to be startable. The closing sentence quotes the owner's own words for his own goal twice, "not the bottleneck on every deliverable" and "without the quality going sideways", both of them straight out of Round 9, and it says "which is how you put it to me" so the reader can hear that they are his. "I believe" is the hedge that keeps a forward-looking sentence a judgment rather than a promise. And nothing in the paragraph is new. Every clause points backward at a section the reader has already read.

Now an illustration. **The company below is fictional, invented for this file, and must never be presented to a client as a real engagement.**

Fernbank Studio, a four-person web design studio. Verdict: `working`. The intake surfaced a shortcut under consideration, so the value finding leads.

> You told me you have been thinking about generating fifty city pages for the site, one for every town in the county, so the studio shows up when somebody searches. I want to say plainly what that is. Those pages are written for a search engine and not for a person, and the person who arrives on one gets a page that pretends the studio knows their town. It would probably work for a while. The ledger on it is that every visitor who reads one learns something about how careful you are, and that record does not fade. You already have the thing those pages are imitating, which is Priya's write-up of the Halloran rebuild, and one of those is worth fifty of the others.
>
> The previous 6 principles suggest the following. Start with the decision you already make well, which is choosing work you can do properly, and say it out loud to the team so it is a standard and not a habit. Write down the discovery call you run in your head, because it is the part of the work that repeats and right now it lives with you. Hold the two-day reply you told me clients count on, especially in the weeks when a build is late. Turn the Halloran rebuild into the case study you have been meaning to write, and let it sit in the open where a stranger can find it. Go back to the three clients from last year who went quiet and find out which ones would come back. Keep watching what the small agencies around you are charging for, since you spotted the retainer shift before anyone else did. All of this is startable this month, and the tools help with every piece of it once you have decided what you are actually making. Through that, I believe you get to "eight retainer clients and no more scrambling in January", which is how you said it to me.

What the illustration is doing: the shortcut is named in the owner's own words, in its own paragraph, without a lecture attached. The consequence is stated once. The synthesis then runs the six in order, in the studio's vocabulary, and closes on the owner's verbatim goal with a hedge in front of it.

## What you hand back

Return the five-part block from the chapter-skill contract.

**verdict**. `strong`, `working`, `thin` or `not observed`, on the value question only. The synthesis is not scored.

**section**. The prose above, ready to drop under `PRINCIPLE 7: Karma & Value`.

**quick_read**. Two or three sentences on the ledger for this company only: the observation, and the move that follows from it. Do not reach into the other six for it. The orchestrator owns the ranking across the laws and writes the Quick Read's closing block that names the highest-leverage law, so naming one here would put two different calls on the same page.

**opportunities**. Usually empty. The synthesis names moves the other six skills already proposed, and duplicating them here corrupts the orchestrator's ranking. Fill this list only when the value finding produced a move that no other section owns, such as declining a specific tactic.

**gaps**. What you could not assess on the ledger, with the questions the owner should answer.

**`blocked_on`**. Optional. The law whose finding has to move first, when this law's recommendation is right but cannot be started until something else changes. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

## Self-check

Before handing the block back, walk this list.

1. No AI product name, company name, or version number anywhere in the section, the quick read, or the opportunities.
2. The synthesis paragraph introduces nothing that the first six sections did not already establish. No new opportunity, observation, or gap.
3. All six earlier principles are named in the paragraph, in law order, one through six.
4. The closing statement uses the owner's own words for their twelve-month goal, verbatim from Round 9, and carries a hedge.
5. The closing statement would not work for any other company. If it would, it is a slogan and it gets rewritten.
6. Only Law 7's own images appear, used once and with weight. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
7. If the intake surfaced a shortcut, it is named plainly, in the owner's words, in its own paragraph, and it has not been softened into a general remark about the market.
8. Any extraction claim traces to a `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` entry. Suspicions went into `gaps` as questions.
9. The section does not moralize. Read it back and cut any sentence that tells the owner what kind of person to be.
10. The last sentence is forward-looking and about this owner's goal, not a manufactured takeaway.
11. First person and second person throughout. No "the client", no "the organization".
12. The section is two to four short paragraphs. It closes the document; it does not restate it.
