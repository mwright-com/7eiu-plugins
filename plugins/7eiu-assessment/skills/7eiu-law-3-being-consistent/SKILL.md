## The law

Whatever a company is to a customer is what it does every time, not the best thing it ever did.

Nothing is still. The table you are leaning on feels solid and it is a storm of motion you cannot see, every particle humming at a frequency that makes the wood wood and not water or smoke. What something is depends on the frequency it holds. A business works the same way. Hold one note steadily for long enough and the market tunes to it, the way a lighthouse bell rung at the same interval on every foggy night lets a fisherman find the harbor without seeing it.

This law governs operations. The reliability of the work. The voice and the quality a customer can count on every single time. The cadence of showing up. The reputation that comes from holding all of that for years, which is the one asset a competitor cannot copy after they have copied everything else.

## What this law is looking for in a business

**Signals the law is obeyed:**

- The owner can say, in one sentence and without thinking about it, what a customer gets from them every time.
- Two different people on the team handle the same request and the customer gets the same answer.
- Something written down governs the work: a checklist, a template, a standard, a script that people actually open.
- Output goes out on a cadence a customer could set a watch by, and it kept going through a busy month.
- Tenure is long, and the long-tenured people are the ones defending the standard rather than working around it.
- Emails, invoices, the site and the intake call sound like the same company.
- When quality slips, somebody catches it before the customer does, and there is a known way to fix it.
- The owner has already decided what stays human, and can name it.

**Signals the law is broken:**

- The note breaks first when the owner gets busy or sick, and everyone knows which part goes first.
- Output happens in bursts and then vanishes for weeks.
- The work sounds like a different company depending on who wrote it.
- The promise a customer can count on has never been decided, only assumed.
- Automation was switched on before the note was chosen, so the company is now reliably broadcasting static.
- The longest-tenured person holds the standard in their head, and it leaves with them.
- New hires learn by sitting next to somebody, and each one learns a different version.
- Quality is described by feel rather than by anything a second person could check.
- The follow-up after the sale depends on whether anyone remembered.
- The company has three ways of doing the same task and no one has picked one.

## What to ask

Round 4 of the intake interview is the core of it. Ask these, listen, and follow the answer.

- What should a customer be able to count on from you, every single time?
- Where does that slip when you get busy, and what breaks first?
- Does your work sound like you across every channel, or does it change depending on who wrote it?
- What is written down, and what lives only in somebody's head?
- Who is the longest-tenured person here, and what do they hold that nobody else does?

Then the probes. Use the ones the answers earn.

- **The ordering question, and the one that matters most here:** has the note been chosen, or are you about to automate before you have decided what you sound like? Ask it plainly. If the owner cannot answer the first question in this round in one sentence, and there is a tool rollout in motion, that is the finding.
- If your longest-tenured person left in the morning, what would a customer notice by Friday?
- When did the cadence last break, and what caused it?
- If I gave the same job to two of your people today, where would the two results differ?
- What have you started and stopped more than once?
- How does a new person learn the job here, and who taught the last one?
- What is already automated, and who decided what it should say?

One more thing to watch for while you listen. Tenure and genuine care about the work show up in this round more than anywhere else in the interview, and they are the strongest evidence you will get that a note is being held. Write down the names and what each person holds, because the section usually opens on them.

Carry the answers into the record marked `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]`. If you did not get an answer, mark it `[gap]` and write it into the section as a question rather than filling it in with something plausible.

## Opportunity patterns

Six moves come up again and again under this law. Each one is startable inside a month.

**1. Name the promise, in writing.**
Missing when the owner answers "what can a customer count on" with three different things across one conversation, or with an adjective.
- *One person:* write one sentence, put it where you see it, and check the last ten things you sent against it.
- *Ten people:* get the sentence agreed in one meeting, then post it where the work happens and use it in the next hire's first week.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* write it before the next location or the next acquisition, because you are about to ask people who have never met you to hold it.

**2. Get the standard out of one person's head.**
Missing when the answer to "what lives only in somebody's head" is a name, and everyone laughs.

Law 3 owns this when the point is that the output is the same every time and a second person can be held to it. When the point is instead that the method can be handed off, run at another size, or sold in a deal, that is Law 2's portability finding, and you name Law 2 in a clause rather than arguing it here.

- *One person:* record yourself doing the job once and turn the recording into a checklist.
- *Ten people:* sit with the tenured person for two hours a week for a month and write down what they do, then have somebody else run the work from the page and fix what the page missed.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* the written standard is the thing you are actually replicating. Build it before the second site, not after.

**3. Hold the cadence on the days your energy would not have.**
Missing when the output history has holes in it that match the busy months.
- *One person:* pick one cadence you can defend, weekly is usually enough, draft ahead with the tools, and keep a backlog two deep.
- *Ten people:* give the cadence an owner who is not the founder, and a calendar the whole team can see.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* hold the cadence during the integration, when it is most likely to break and most likely to be noticed.

**4. Decide what gets systematized and what stays human.**
Missing when the owner is either automating everything or refusing to automate anything.
- *One person:* list the repeated tasks, mark the ones a customer would never want a person for, and take those first.
- *Ten people:* draw the line in a meeting and write it down, then hold it. The frequency gets systematized. The soul does not. Law 6 owns the outward positioning question of what a market pays a person for, and Law 3 owns the inward one, which is what stays human inside your own operation.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* protect the human moments by name, because scale erodes the ones nobody named.

**5. Make the voice one voice across every channel.**
Missing when the intake email, the site and the invoice read like three companies, or when the owner says a customer would be able to tell who wrote which.
- *One person:* put ten things you have sent side by side, keep the two that sound most like you, and write down what they have in common.
- *Ten people:* build the shared examples into templates people start from, so the default is your voice and the exception is somebody's own.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* the acquired team already has a voice. Decide which one survives before the first joint message goes out.

**6. Catch the inconsistency before it gets scaled.**
Missing when growth is about to run ahead of what is written down, which is the most common version of this law breaking in a company that is otherwise doing fine.
- *One person:* before you take the volume, run one week at the new volume and see what drops.
- *Ten people:* look at the last five complaints and ask whether each one is a person problem or a missing standard.
- *Scaling or acquiring:* pause on the thing you are about to replicate and ask whether it is good enough to have ten copies of.

## The AI angle

Describe what the tools do. Never name a product, a company, or a version number. A shipped draft that names one is a hard fail.

Three things belong in this section when the finding calls for them.

The tools are unusually good at holding a frequency a tired person cannot hold alone. Steady output. Follow-up that happens every time. A voice that stays recognizable across a thousand small messages written on days when nobody had the energy to write each one from scratch. For a small operation this is arguably the single most valuable thing they do, and it is worth saying to an owner who has been trying to hold the note with willpower.

The tools can only hold a note that has already been chosen. An owner who automates before deciding gets consistency they will regret, because the machine will broadcast whatever it was given, reliably and at scale. Order of operations is the whole finding here. Choose the note, then automate its manufacture.

There is a line past which a customer can feel that the care itself has been automated, and once they feel it the trust starts to go. So the judgment stays human. The presence stays human. The moment a customer needs a person and not a process stays human. Say which moments those are for this company, using their own words for them.

## Writing the PRINCIPLE 3 section

Heading: `PRINCIPLE 3: Be Consistent`

Length: one to three short paragraphs. Do not pad it to match the long sections. Three to six sentences a paragraph.

Use the four moves from the benchmark, and not all four are required: the observation, attributed to where it came from; the law applied in its own terms without being quoted at the reader; the opportunity at the size the company actually is; the honest gap, written as italic questions.

Open on the observation, and where you can, open on the people. This law lives in what the team does every day, so the first sentence is usually something the owner told you about how the work gets done or who holds it. Two openings work well. One is the thing a customer gets every time, quoted back in the owner's words. The other is the moment the note breaks, named without drama. Do not open by defining consistency, and do not open with a compliment that has no observation under it.

This section frequently reads as an affirmation rather than a correction. A company with tenure and people who genuinely care about the work is often already holding a note, and the honest finding is that it is holding. When that is what you found, say so plainly and tell the owner to keep doing it. Then name the one condition under which it would break, which is usually growth arriving faster than the standards and the tools that would carry it. That single condition is the value of the section. An affirmation with a named breaking point is a finding. An affirmation without one is flattery.

Hedge to what you learned. "it seems", "I suspect", "may be", "From my vantage point". First person and second person throughout. Their vocabulary, verbatim.

**Vocabulary this law owns:** frequency, the note, the bell, the fog, entrainment, holding steady, the interval, tuning, resonance, static.

**Not yours. Every image below belongs to another law:**

"Scale" in the ordinary sense, the size of a company or work that scales, is plain English and every law may use it. Only the figures are owned: Law 2's snowflake-against-glacier scale and Law 5's continuous-temperature scale. "Static" is this law's alone.

## Worked example

The benchmark's PRINCIPLE 3 section read in full:

> Because of the leadership within the office, in terms of tenure and genuine concern about how the business performs, consistency with your offering will only become an issue if growth is introduced faster than the tools that will enable it. This is good, because the act of embracing the possibility of more efficient solutions sets the tone going forward. Regarding the third principle, keep doing what you're doing. This act of stimulating progress in a way that is beneficial internally and externally in order to deliver consistent results across a variety of client types and infrastructure can set the bar for the industry. This may be what Clear RCM becomes known for.

What it is doing, line by line:

It opens on the observation, and the observation is about people rather than process. Tenure and genuine concern are things the author saw in the room, so they can carry the paragraph.

It names one condition under which the note would break, growth arriving faster than the tools, and it does not invent a second one. There was no problem to report, so no problem was manufactured.

"Keep doing what you're doing" is stated without apology and without a softening clause after it. That is a legitimate verdict and the document treats it as one.

The law is used rather than quoted. The word consistency appears once. Nobody gets told what a frequency is.

The opportunity is sized to a company that is about to grow, and it is expressed in the client's own terms: a variety of client types and infrastructure.

It ends on a forward-looking possibility about reputation, which is exactly what this law is about over years, and not on a summary line.

**An illustration. Bishop and Barrow is invented for teaching purposes. It is not a client, and nothing in it happened.** A two-location dog grooming business, verdict `thin`.

> You told me the Northgate shop and the Riverside shop do the same work, and then you told me that a customer who books a full groom at Riverside gets a nail trim included and at Northgate does not, because that is how Dee has always done it. Neither of those is wrong. What is missing is a decision about which one is the promise. Right now a customer who moves between your two shops gets two companies, and you are about to open a third.
>
> There may be an opportunity here that costs you an afternoon. Write down the one thing a customer gets from you every single time, decide whether the nail trim is in it, and get that sentence in front of both shops before you sign the next lease. From my vantage point the care is already there in both places, so this is about picking the note rather than raising the standard. I would also get what Dee knows onto paper this quarter, because eleven years of it is currently held in one person.
>
> *What does a customer get every time, in one sentence?*
> *If Dee were out for a month, what would a regular notice first?*


`verdict` is one of `strong`, `working`, `thin`, `not observed`. Write `quick_read` fresh rather than trimming it out of `section`. Propose a `diagram` only if this law carries the biggest opportunity in the whole engagement, and let the orchestrator decide.

Add `blocked_on` when the recommendation here is right but cannot be started until something else changes. It names the law whose finding has to move first. This is sequencing, not an excuse. Name the law that has to move and why, and keep the recommendation as it stands.

Check `quick_read` against the intake record before you return it. It is written last, when the writing is going well, and it is where a number or a duration that nobody said tends to appear.

## Self-check

Run these before handing back.

1. No AI product name, company name, or version number appears anywhere.
2. Every claim traces to a `[said]`, `[doc]` or `[observed]` entry in the intake. Nothing plausible was filled in.
3. If the finding is an affirmation, it is stated plainly, it is not padded into a false problem, and it names the one condition under which the note would break.
4. Only Law 3's own images appear. Checked against the full table above, not from memory.
5. The finding sits in operations. Anything about reaching strangers went to Law 4.
6. The recommendation names the specific promise, standard or cadence. It never says "be more consistent".
7. Every recommendation is startable inside a month at this company's actual size.
8. The section ends on its content, a question, or what happens next. No manufactured takeaway.
9. First person and second person throughout. No "the client", no "the organization".
10. Claims are hedged to what was learned. The confidence is in the specifics.
11. Gaps are written into the section as italic questions, not hidden.
12. Length is one to three short paragraphs, and it was not padded to match a longer section.
